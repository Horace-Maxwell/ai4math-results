\begin{abstract}
Jiang and Yang determined the maximal energy of integral circulant graphs of
order $n=p^{2r}q^{2s+1}$, where $p,q$ are distinct odd primes with $q\ge5$:
the checkerboard divisor set $\{p^iq^j: i+j \text{ even}\}$ is the unique
maximiser. They noted that their semidefinite method does not extend to
$q=3$. We prove the remaining case: for every prime $p\ge5$ and all $r\ge1$,
$s\ge0$, the checkerboard set is the unique energy maximiser for order
$p^{2r}3^{2s+1}$, with the maximal energy given by the formula of Jiang and
Yang. This settles the maximal-energy problem for opposite-parity exponents
for all pairs of distinct odd primes. The key step is a sharp inequality for
sign matrices under weighted Ramanujan transforms. We prove it with a path
identity and an explicit potential function, and the same argument gives a
semidefinite-free proof of the theorem of Jiang and Yang. For exponents of
equal parity the checkerboard set contains $n$ and is not admissible. We
determine the maximal energy, and its two maximisers, when one of the exponents
is $1$ or $2$, for all distinct odd primes $p,q$, including $3$, the other
exponent being arbitrary; for $n=pq$ it also follows from known energy formulas.
For example, for $n=p^2q^b$ with $b$ even the maximal energy is
$\frac12[n+(5p^2-8p+4)\,d_b(q)]-(p^2-2p+2)\,\delta_b(q)$, where $d_b$ and
$\delta_b$ are explicit polynomials, and it is attained exactly by
$\{p^iq^j: i+j\text{ odd}\}$ and $\{p^iq^j: i+j\text{ even}\}\setminus\{n\}$.
We also prove the formula and the two maximisers when one of the primes is~$3$
and both exponents are at least~$3$, the other prime and the exponents being
arbitrary; the proof verifies, for the prime~$3$, every exponent and every other odd prime,
the conditions of the conditional reduction of version~2
(Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}). So the equal-parity formula holds for every order $p^aq^b$
with $\min\{a,b\}\le2$ and for every order of equal parity in which one of the
primes is~$3$; with the opposite-parity case, this determines the maximal energy
for all such orders. Version~1.5 of this paper claimed the range
$3\le\min\{a,b\}\le8$ by a computer-assisted argument without a formal proof;
version~2 withdrew that claim. Jiang and Yang~\cite{jy2} have stated the same
formula, $\frac12[n+d_a(p)d_b(q)]-\delta_a(p)\delta_b(q)$, and the same two
maximisers, for all exponents of equal parity and distinct primes $p,q\ge5$. Their
statement covers the remaining orders. We have not reviewed its proof, and it is
not formally verified. If it is correct, the two results together determine the
maximal energy for every order $p^aq^b$ with distinct odd primes $p,q$. All the
mathematics of this paper,
including the closed forms of $d_k$ and $\delta_k$, is formally verified in
Lean~4; statements about the literature are not. The proofs were found by AI
coding agents working under the author's direction; their role is described at
the end of the paper. No human mathematician has reviewed this paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The energy of a finite graph is the sum of the absolute values of its
adjacency eigenvalues~\cite{gutman}. For $n\ge2$ and a nonempty set
$\mathcal D$ of proper divisors of $n$, the integral circulant graph
$\ICG_n(\mathcal D)$ has vertex set $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$, two distinct
vertices $u,v$ being adjacent when $\gcd(u-v,n)\in\mathcal D$. These are the
circulant graphs with integral spectrum~\cite{so}. The problem of maximising
the energy of $\ICG_n(\mathcal D)$ over $\mathcal D$ for fixed $n$ has been
solved for prime powers~\cite{sander2013} and studied for other orders; see
the introduction of~\cite{jy} for a survey. Rold\'an~\cite{roldan} conjectured
that for $n=p^2q^3$ the checkerboard set $\{1,p^2,pq,q^2,p^2q^2,pq^3\}$ is the
unique maximiser. Jiang and Yang~\cite{jy} proved much more.

\begin{theorem}[Jiang--Yang {\cite[Theorem~A]{jy}}]\label{thm:jy}
Let $p,q$ be distinct odd primes with $q\ge5$, let $r\ge1$, $s\ge0$ and
$n=p^{2r}q^{2s+1}$. Among all nonempty sets of proper divisors of $n$, the
energy of $\ICG_n(\mathcal D)$ is uniquely maximised by
\[
 \mathcal D^*_{r,s}=\{p^iq^j:\ 0\le i\le 2r,\ 0\le j\le 2s+1,\ i+j\ \text{even}\},
\]
and the maximal energy is $\tfrac12\bigl[n+d_{2r}(p)\,d_{2s+1}(q)\bigr]$, where
$d_k(x)=(2k+1)x^k+4\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{k-j}(j+1)x^j$.
\end{theorem}

They remark that the case $q=3$ is not covered, because their odd-exponent
semidefinite argument fails at $x=3$~\cite[Remark~4.2]{jy}. Our main result
removes this restriction.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:jy} holds for $q=3$: for every prime
$p\ge5$ and all $r\ge1$, $s\ge0$, the set $\mathcal D^*_{r,s}$ is the unique
energy maximiser among nonempty sets of proper divisors of $n=p^{2r}3^{2s+1}$,
and the maximal energy is $\tfrac12\bigl[n+d_{2r}(p)\,d_{2s+1}(3)\bigr]$.
\end{theorem}

Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:jy} (which allows $p=3$ on the even side),
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} determines the maximal energy and the unique maximiser
for every order $p^{\mathrm{even}}q^{\mathrm{odd}}$ with $p,q$ distinct odd
primes. The case $r=s=1$ was treated earlier. Park~\cite{park} claims a proof of
Rold\'an's conjecture for all distinct odd primes. Schreib~\cite{schreib}
gives a written proof of the case $q=3$ and combines it with~\cite{jy} to
obtain the conjecture.
An arithmetic certificate for $n=27p^2$ was announced in~\cite{tru}. These
works concern only the exponents $(2,3)$, and, when Theorem~\ref{thm:main} was
released in version~1.1.0 of~\cite{repo} on 26 September 2026, we were not aware
of any earlier treatment of the case $q=3$ for general $r$ and $s$.

If the two exponents have the same parity, the checkerboard set contains $n$
itself and is not a set of proper divisors, and the maximiser changes.
Conjecture~\ref{conj:equal}, stated in earlier versions of this paper, gives two
maximisers and an explicit maximal energy for this case. We prove it when one of
the exponents equals~$1$ or~$2$, and, in version~3, when one of the primes
equals~$3$. For $n=p^aq^b$, a set written as
$\{p^iq^j:\ \dots\}$ consists of the divisors $p^iq^j$ of $n$ ($0\le i\le a$,
$0\le j\le b$) with the stated property, and
$\delta_k(x)=\bigl(x^k(x-1)+2(-1)^k\bigr)/(x+1)$ (Remark~\ref{rem:closed}).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:onem}
Let $p,q$ be distinct odd primes, let $m\ge1$ be odd, and let $n=pq^m$. The
maximal energy of an integral circulant graph of order $n$ is
\[
 \tfrac12\bigl[n+(3p-4)\,d_m(q)\bigr]-(p-2)\,\delta_m(q),
\]
and it is attained exactly by the sets $\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{odd}\}$ and
$\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{even}\}\setminus\{n\}$. Exchanging the names of the
primes, for $n=p^mq$ the maximal energy is
$\tfrac12\bigl[n+(3q-4)\,d_m(p)\bigr]-(q-2)\,\delta_m(p)$, attained exactly by
the two sets with the same description.
\end{theorem}

For $m=1$ the maximal energy is $4(p-1)(q-1)$, attained by $\{p,q\}$ and
$\{1\}$. This case can also be derived from published energy formulas of
Ili\'c~\cite{ilic} and of Ili\'c and Ba\v{s}i\'c~\cite{ilicbasic}
(Remark~\ref{rem:pq}), although we have not found it stated. For the
exponent $2$ we prove the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ptwo}
Let $p,q$ be distinct odd primes, let $b\ge2$ be even, and let $n=p^2q^b$.
The maximal energy of an integral circulant graph of order $n$ is
\[
 \tfrac12\bigl[n+(5p^2-8p+4)\,d_b(q)\bigr]-(p^2-2p+2)\,\delta_b(q),
\]
and it is attained exactly by the sets $\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{odd}\}$ and
$\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{even}\}\setminus\{n\}$. Exchanging the names of the
primes, for $n=p^bq^2$ the maximal energy is
$\tfrac12\bigl[n+(5q^2-8q+4)\,d_b(p)\bigr]-(q^2-2q+2)\,\delta_b(p)$, attained
exactly by the two sets with the same description.
\end{theorem}

Here $5x^2-8x+4=d_2(x)$ and $x^2-2x+2=\delta_2(x)$. The proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ptwo} gives a sign-matrix inequality for all real $p\ge5$,
$q\ge3$ and for $p=3$, $q\ge5$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:signB}). Since also
$3x-4=d_1(x)$ and $x-2=\delta_1(x)$, Theorems~\ref{thm:onem} and~\ref{thm:ptwo}
together give the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:minfour}
Let $p,q$ be distinct odd primes, let $a,b\ge1$ with $a+b$ even, suppose that
$\min\{a,b\}\le2$ or that one of $p,q$ equals~$3$, and let $n=p^aq^b$. The
maximal energy of an integral circulant graph of order $n$ is
$\tfrac12\bigl[n+d_a(p)\,d_b(q)\bigr]-\delta_a(p)\,\delta_b(q)$, and it is
attained exactly by the sets $\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{odd}\}$ and
$\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{even}\}\setminus\{n\}$.
\end{theorem}

This is Theorem~\ref{thm:onem} if $\min\{a,b\}=1$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:ptwo} if
$\min\{a,b\}=2$; in these cases the other exponent is arbitrary, and the prime~$3$
is allowed. If $\min\{a,b\}\ge3$ and one of the primes is~$3$, the theorem is
proved in Section~\ref{sec:three}, with no bound on the exponents or on the other
prime. Version~1.5 stated this theorem for $\min\{a,b\}\le8$, and version~2 for
$\min\{a,b\}\le2$; see the footnote on the title page.

Theorems~\ref{thm:jy} and~\ref{thm:main} cover every order whose exponents
have opposite parity. So Theorems~\ref{thm:jy}--\ref{thm:minfour} determine
the maximal energy, and all maximisers, for every order $p^aq^b$ with
$\min\{a,b\}\le2$, for every order $3^aq^b$, and for every order whose exponents
have opposite parity, where $p,q$ are distinct odd primes.

\section{Weighted Ramanujan transforms}
We use the notation of~\cite{jy}. For real $x>1$, an integer $k\ge1$ and
$0\le i,j\le k$, let $T_k(x)=(t_{ij})$ be the symmetric matrix with
\[
t_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,& i+j\le k-2,\\ -x^{k-1}(x-1),& i+j=k-1,\\
x^{2k-i-j-2}(x-1)^2,& i+j\ge k,\end{cases}\qquad (i,j<k),
\]
$t_{ik}=t_{ki}=x^{k-i-1}(x-1)$ for $i<k$, and $t_{kk}=1$. When $x$ is prime,
$t_{ij}=\varphi(x^{k-i})\,c_{x^{k-j}}(x^i)$, where $c_m(u)$ denotes
Ramanujan's sum~\cite[(2.2)--(2.4)]{jy}. The row sums are
$T_k(x)\mathbf 1=x^ke_k$~\cite[(2.5)]{jy}, where $e_k$ is the last standard
basis vector. For a real matrix $Z$ let $\|Z\|_1$ be the sum of the absolute
values of its entries.

\begin{proposition}[{\cite[Proposition~2.2]{jy}}]\label{prop:model}
Let $p\ne q$ be primes, $a,b\ge1$, $n=p^aq^b$, and let $X\in\{0,1\}^{(a+1)
\times(b+1)}$ have $x_{ij}=1$ exactly when $p^iq^j\in\mathcal D$. Then
$\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D))=\|T_a(p)XT_b(q)^{\mathsf T}\|_1$.
\end{proposition}

Let $s_k=(1,-1,1,\dots,(-1)^k)^{\mathsf T}$ and define
$d_k(x)=\|T_k(x)s_k\|_1$, and put $d_0(x)=1$. By Remark~\ref{rem:closed}
below, $d_k(x)$ agrees with the closed form in Theorem~\ref{thm:jy} for all
real $x>2$; see also~\cite[(3.6)]{jy}.

\section{A path identity}\label{sec:path}
For $w\in\mathbb R^{k+1}$ define $z_{k-1}=w_k$ and, for
$j=k-1,k-2,\dots,0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:path}
z_{j-1}=\frac{z_j+(x-1)w_j}{x}.
\end{equation}
Thus $z_j$ is a convex combination of $w_{j+1},\dots,w_k$, and each $z_j$ is a
linear function of $w$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:path}
For $w\in\mathbb R^{k+1}$ we have
\[
\|T_k(x)w\|_1=x^k\Bigl(|z_{-1}|+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|z_{j-1}-z_j|\Bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Unwinding~\eqref{eq:path} gives
$z_j=x^{-(k-j-1)}\sum_{i>j}\varphi(x^{k-i})w_i$ with $\varphi(x^0)=1$ and
$\varphi(x^e)=x^{e-1}(x-1)$. The last row of $T_k(x)$ is
$(\varphi(x^{k-i}))_i$, so $(T_k(x)w)_k=x^kz_{-1}$. For $u<k$ put
$j=k-1-u$. Row $u$ has entry $-x^{k-1}(x-1)$ in column $j$, entries
$x^{j}(x-1)\varphi(x^{k-i})$ in columns $i>j$, and zeros elsewhere. Hence
$(T_k(x)w)_u=x^{k-1}(x-1)(z_j-w_j)$, while
$z_{j-1}-z_j=\frac{x-1}{x}(w_j-z_j)$.
\end{proof}

\section{The one-dimensional potential}\label{sec:potential}
For $x>2$ let $\mu_{-1}=1$, $\mu_j=(x-1-\mu_{j-1})/x$ for $j\ge0$, and
$c_j=(x-1)(1+\mu_{j-1})/x$. By induction,
$\mu_j\in\bigl[\frac{x-2}{x},1\bigr]$ for all $j$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:potential}
Let $x>2$, let $y\in\{\pm1\}^{k+1}$, and let $z_j$ be defined
by~\eqref{eq:path} with $w=y$. Then $\operatorname{sgn} z_j=y_{j+1}$ and
$\frac{x-2}{x}\le|z_j|\le1$ for $-1\le j\le k-1$, and
\[
\frac{\|T_k(x)y\|_1}{x^k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_j+\mu_{k-1}
 -\sum_{j\in\NA(y)}2\mu_j|z_j|,\qquad
\NA(y)=\{0\le j\le k-1:\ y_j=y_{j+1}\}.
\]
Consequently $\|T_k(x)y\|_1\le d_k(x)$, with equality exactly when
$y=\pm s_k$, and $d_k(x)=x^k(\sum_jc_j+\mu_{k-1})$. Moreover
$(T_k(x)s_k)_k>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We have $z_{k-1}=y_k$, and since $x-1>1\ge|z_j|$, the sign of
$z_{j-1}=(z_j+(x-1)y_j)/x$ is $y_j$ and
$|z_{j-1}|=(x-1\pm|z_j|)/x\in[\frac{x-2}{x},1]$. Put
$f_j=|z_{j-1}-z_j|+\mu_{j-1}|z_{j-1}|-\mu_j|z_j|$. If $y_j=-\operatorname{sgn}z_j$,
then $|z_{j-1}-z_j|=\frac{x-1}{x}(1+|z_j|)$ and
$|z_{j-1}|=\frac{x-1-|z_j|}{x}$; using $x\mu_j=x-1-\mu_{j-1}$ one finds
$f_j=c_j$. If $y_j=\operatorname{sgn}z_j$, the same computation with
$|z_{j-1}-z_j|=\frac{x-1}{x}(1-|z_j|)$ and $|z_{j-1}|=\frac{x-1+|z_j|}{x}$
gives $f_j=c_j-2\mu_j|z_j|$. Summing, the terms $\mu_j|z_j|$ telescope, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:path} gives the identity, since $\mu_{-1}=1$ and
$|z_{k-1}|=1$. Every $j\in\NA(y)$ contributes a positive term
$2\mu_j|z_j|$, and $\NA(y)=\emptyset$ exactly for $y=\pm s_k$. Finally
$(T_k(x)s_k)_k=x^kz_{-1}$ and $\operatorname{sgn}z_{-1}=(s_k)_0=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:closed}
Put $\delta_k(x)=x^k\mu_{k-1}$. Solving the recursion for $\mu$ gives
$\delta_k(x)=\bigl(x^k(x-1)+2(-1)^k\bigr)/(x+1)
=x^k+2\sum_{i<k}(-1)^{k-i}x^i$. For $y=s_k$, every step in
the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} has $y_j=-\operatorname{sgn}z_j$, so
$|z_{j-1}|=(x-1-|z_j|)/x$; starting from $|z_{k-1}|=1=\mu_{-1}$ this gives
$|z_j|=\mu_{k-2-j}$, and in particular
$(T_k(x)s_k)_k=x^kz_{-1}=\delta_k(x)$. Since the potential does not
depend on $k$, Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} gives
$d_{k+1}/x^{k+1}-d_k/x^k=c_k+\mu_k-\mu_{k-1}=2\mu_k$, that is,
$d_{k+1}(x)=x\,d_k(x)+2\delta_{k+1}(x)$, with $d_0=1$. The closed form
$(2k+1)x^k+4\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{k-j}(j+1)x^j$ satisfies the same recursion
and initial value, so it equals $d_k(x)$ for all $k\ge0$.
\end{remark}

\section{A two-variable inequality}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cell}
Let $P\ge\mu$ and $0\le\mu\le1$, and put
$h(A,B)=P|A-B|+\mu|B+PA|-(P-\mu)|B|-P(1+\mu)|A|$ for real $A,B$. Then
$h(A,B)\le2\mu\max\{0,|B|-P|A|\}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
As $h(-A,-B)=h(A,B)$ we may assume $A\ge0$. If $0\le B\le A$ then
$h=-2(P-\mu)B\le0$. If $B>A$ then $h=2(\mu B-PA)\le2\mu(B-PA)$ because
$\mu\le1$. If $-PA\le B<0$ then $h=0$. If $B<-PA$ then $h=2\mu(|B|-PA)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:local}
Let $q\ge3$, $b\ge1$, $N=T_b(q)$, $F(w)=\|Nw\|_1$ and $D=d_b(q)$. Let
$P\ge4$ and $\frac35\le\mu\le1$. For all $y\in\{\pm1\}^{b+1}$ and
$\zeta\in[-1,1]^{b+1}$,
\[
\Lambda(y,\zeta):=P\,F(y-\zeta)+\mu\,F(\zeta+Py)-(P-\mu)\,F(\zeta)
\le P(1+\mu)\,D,
\]
with strict inequality unless $y=\pm s_b$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $\xi_j(w)$ be the path~\eqref{eq:path} with $x=q$, $k=b$, and put
$\tau_{-1}(w)=\xi_{-1}(w)$, $\tau_j(w)=\xi_{j-1}(w)-\xi_j(w)$ for $0\le j<b$.
These are linear, and $F(w)=q^b\sum_j|\tau_j(w)|$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:path}.
With $A_j=\tau_j(y)$ and $B_j=\tau_j(\zeta)$,
\[
\Lambda/q^b=P(1+\mu)F(y)/q^b+\textstyle\sum_jh(A_j,B_j).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} (with $x=q$ and potential $\mu^q_j$), it
suffices to show $\sum_jh(A_j,B_j)\le\sum_{j\in\NA(y)}2P(1+\mu)\mu^q_jx_j$,
where $x_j=|\xi_j(y)|\in[\frac{q-2}{q},1]$, with strict inequality when
$\NA(y)\ne\emptyset$. Since $\xi_j(\zeta)$ is a convex combination of entries
of $\zeta$, we have $|B_{-1}|\le1$ and
$|B_j|=\frac{q-1}{q}|\zeta_j-\xi_j(\zeta)|\le\frac{2(q-1)}{q}$. Also
$|A_{-1}|\ge\frac{q-2}{q}$, and $|A_j|=\frac{q-1}{q}(1+x_j)$ for
$j\notin\NA(y)$, $|A_j|=\frac{q-1}{q}(1-x_j)$ for $j\in\NA(y)$. We use
Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}, noting $P\ge4>\mu$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $j=-1$: $P|A_{-1}|\ge\frac{4(q-2)}{q}\ge\frac43>|B_{-1}|$, so $h\le0$.
\item $j\ge0$, $j\notin\NA(y)$: $P|A_j|\ge\frac{8(q-1)^2}{q^2}\ge
 \frac{2(q-1)}{q}\ge|B_j|$, so $h\le0$.
\item $j\in\NA(y)$: it suffices that $\mu|B_j|<P\beta(x_j)$, where
 $\beta(t)=\mu\frac{q-1}{q}(1-t)+(1+\mu)\mu^q_jt$. As $\beta$ is affine it
 suffices to check the endpoints. For $q=3$, $\beta(1)\ge\frac85\cdot\frac13$
 and $\beta(\frac13)\ge\frac35\cdot\frac49+\frac85\cdot\frac19=\frac49$, so
 $P\beta\ge\frac{16}{9}>\frac43\ge\mu|B_j|$. For general $q\ge3$ the same
 comparison reduces to $P\beta(1)\ge\frac{32}{5}\cdot\frac{q-2}{q}>
 \frac{2(q-1)}{q}$ and $\frac{22}5q^2-\frac{94}5q+\frac{104}5>0$.
\end{itemize}
Summing over $j$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\section{The sign-matrix inequality}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:sign}
Let $p\ge5$ and $q\ge3$ be real, $a,b\ge1$, $M=T_a(p)$ and $N=T_b(q)$. For
every $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}$,
\[
\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1\le d_a(p)\,d_b(q),
\]
with equality exactly when $Y=\pm s_as_b^{\mathsf T}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\bar y_0,\dots,\bar y_a$ be the rows of $Y$. Define $\zeta_{a-1}=\bar
y_a$ and $\zeta_{k-1}=(\zeta_k+(p-1)\bar y_k)/p\in[-1,1]^{b+1}$. Column $v$ of
$MYN^{\mathsf T}$ is $Mw^{(v)}$ with $w^{(v)}_i=(N\bar y_i)_v$, and by
linearity the path of $w^{(v)}$ is $((N\zeta_k)_v)_k$. Applying
Lemma~\ref{lem:path} with $x=p$ to every column and summing,
\[
\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1=p^a\Bigl(F(\zeta_{-1})+\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}
F(\zeta_{k-1}-\zeta_k)\Bigr),\qquad F(w)=\|Nw\|_1.
\]
Let $\mu_k$ be the potential of Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} for $x=p$ and put
$f_k=F(\zeta_{k-1}-\zeta_k)+\mu_{k-1}F(\zeta_{k-1})-\mu_kF(\zeta_k)$.
Telescoping gives
$\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1/p^a=\sum_kf_k+\mu_{a-1}F(\bar y_a)$. Since
$\zeta_{k-1}-\zeta_k=\frac{p-1}{p}(\bar y_k-\zeta_k)$ and
$p\mu_k=p-1-\mu_{k-1}$, we get $pf_k=\Lambda(\bar y_k,\zeta_k)$ with
$P=p-1\ge4$ and $\mu=\mu_{k-1}\in[\frac{p-2}{p},1]\subseteq[\frac35,1]$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:local} gives $f_k\le c_kd_b(q)$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} gives $F(\bar y_a)\le d_b(q)$; both are strict
unless the row involved is $\pm s_b$, and $\mu_{a-1}>0$. Hence
$\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1\le p^a(\sum_kc_k+\mu_{a-1})d_b(q)=d_a(p)d_b(q)$.
If equality holds, every row of $Y$ is $\pm s_b$, so $Y=gs_b^{\mathsf T}$ with
$g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1=\|Mg\|_1\,d_b(q)$; by
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} this equals $d_a(p)d_b(q)$ only for $g=\pm s_a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:swap}
Since $\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1=\|NY^{\mathsf T}M^{\mathsf T}\|_1$, the roles of
the two factors may be exchanged, so Theorem~\ref{thm:sign} holds whenever
$\min(p,q)\ge3$ and $\max(p,q)\ge5$. In particular it contains the sign-matrix
inequality~\cite[Lemma~4.1]{jy} used for Theorem~\ref{thm:jy}, and the
deduction below then proves Theorem~\ref{thm:jy} as well. The semidefinite
argument of~\cite{jy} does not extend to $x=3$~\cite[Remark~4.2]{jy}; the
proof above instead uses the integrality of the signs on both factors.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}\label{sec:proofmain}
Let $a=2r$, $b=2s+1$, $M=T_a(p)$, $N=T_b(3)$, let $X$ be the divisor matrix
of $\mathcal D$ (so $x_{ab}=0$), and let $Y=2X-J$ with $J$ the all-ones
matrix. By Proposition~\ref{prop:model} and $M\mathbf 1=p^ae_a$,
$N\mathbf1=3^be_b$,
\[
2\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D))=\|MJN^{\mathsf T}+MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1
=\|n\,e_ae_b^{\mathsf T}+MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1\le n+d_a(p)\,d_b(3),
\]
by Theorem~\ref{thm:sign}. For the checkerboard set, $Y^*=s_as_b^{\mathsf T}$,
$x^*_{00}=1$, and $x^*_{ab}=0$ because $a+b$ is odd, so $\mathcal D^*_{r,s}$
is an admissible nonempty set. The $(a,b)$ entry of
$MY^*N^{\mathsf T}=(Ms_a)(Ns_b)^{\mathsf T}$ is positive by
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential}, so it does not cancel against $n$, and
$2\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D^*_{r,s}))=n+\|Ms_a\|_1\|Ns_b\|_1=n+d_a(p)\,d_b(3)$. If
$\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D))=\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D^*_{r,s}))$, then
$\|MYN^{\mathsf T}\|_1=d_a(p)d_b(3)$, so $Y=\pm s_as_b^{\mathsf T}$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:sign}; since $y_{ab}=-1=(s_as_b^{\mathsf T})_{ab}$, we get
$Y=Y^*$ and $\mathcal D=\mathcal D^*_{r,s}$. \qed

\section{Exponents of equal parity}\label{sec:equal}
Let $p,q$ be distinct odd primes, let $a,b\ge1$ with $a+b$ even, $n=p^aq^b$,
$M=T_a(p)$ and $N=T_b(q)$. For a set $\mathcal D$ of proper divisors of $n$
let $X$ and $Y=2X-J$ be as in Section~\ref{sec:proofmain}, so that
$y_{ab}=-1$, and let $Z=MYN^{\mathsf T}$, with entries $Z_{ij}$. The last rows
of $M$ and $N$ are nonnegative with sums $p^a$ and $q^b$, so $|Z_{ab}|\le n$.
Writing $t^-=\max\{-t,0\}$, the computation of Section~\ref{sec:proofmain}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G}
G(Y):=2\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D))-n=\sum_{(i,j)\ne(a,b)}|Z_{ij}|+Z_{ab}
=\|Z\|_1-2(Z_{ab})^-.
\end{equation}

Since $a+b$ is even, the sign matrix $s_as_b^{\mathsf T}$ has entry $+1$ at
$(a,b)$ and violates the proper-divisor condition. The matrix
$-s_as_b^{\mathsf T}$, which corresponds to the set $\{p^iq^j: i+j\text{
odd}\}$, is admissible, and it attains the bound $\|Z\|_1\le d_a(p)\,d_b(q)$
of Theorem~\ref{thm:sign}. However, its entry
$Z_{ab}=-(Ms_a)_a(Ns_b)_b=-\delta_a(p)\,\delta_b(q)$ is negative by
Remark~\ref{rem:closed}, and it cancels against $n$ in the argument of
Section~\ref{sec:proofmain}. So the energy bound
$\tfrac12[n+d_a(p)\,d_b(q)]$ is not attained, and the maximiser changes.
By~\eqref{eq:G}, the set $\{p^iq^j: i+j\text{ odd}\}$ has energy
$\tfrac12[n+d_a(p)\,d_b(q)]-\delta_a(p)\,\delta_b(q)$.

\begin{conjecture}[stated in earlier versions; proved here when $\min\{a,b\}\le2$ or one of the primes is~$3$]\label{conj:equal}
Let $p,q$ be distinct odd primes, $a,b\ge1$ with $a+b$ even, and
$n=p^aq^b$. The maximal energy of an integral circulant graph of order $n$ is
\[
\tfrac12\bigl[n+d_a(p)\,d_b(q)\bigr]-\delta_a(p)\,\delta_b(q),
\]
attained exactly by the sets $\{p^iq^j: i+j\text{ odd}\}$ and
$\{p^iq^j: i+j\text{ even}\}\setminus\{n\}$.
\end{conjecture}


By the computation above, the first of the two sets always attains the
conjectured value. Since $d_1(x)=3x-4$, $\delta_1(x)=x-2$,
$d_2(x)=5x^2-8x+4$ and $\delta_2(x)=x^2-2x+2$, Theorems~\ref{thm:onem}
and~\ref{thm:ptwo} are the cases $\min\{a,b\}=1$ and $\min\{a,b\}=2$ of the
conjecture, proved in Sections~\ref{sec:onem} and~\ref{sec:ptwo}.
Section~\ref{sec:three} proves the case in which one of the primes is~$3$ and
$\min\{a,b\}\ge3$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:minfour} collects these cases. So the
conjecture is proved here, with Lean proofs, whenever $\min\{a,b\}\le2$ or one of
the primes is~$3$. The remaining orders, with $p,q\ge5$ and $\min\{a,b\}\ge3$, are
covered by the statement of~\cite[Theorem~A]{jy2}. We have not reviewed its proof,
it is not formally verified, and nothing in our proofs uses it. If it is correct,
the conjecture holds for all pairs of distinct odd primes.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:pq}
For $n=pq$, Theorem~\ref{thm:onem} gives the maximal energy $4(p-1)(q-1)$,
attained exactly by $\{p,q\}$ and $\{1\}$. This can also be read off from
known formulas. The graph $\ICG_{pq}(\{1\})$ is the unitary Cayley graph,
whose energy $4\varphi(pq)$ was computed by Ili\'c~\cite{ilic}, and
$\E(\ICG_{pq}(\{p,q\}))=4\varphi(pq)$ by~\cite[Theorem~4.2]{ilicbasic}. The
graphs $\ICG_{pq}(\{p\})$ and $\ICG_{pq}(\{1,p\})$ are $p$ disjoint copies of
$K_q$ and the complete $q$-partite graph with parts of size~$p$
(see also~\cite[Theorem~4.1]{ilicbasic}); both have energy $2p(q-1)$, and
symmetrically for $\{q\}$ and $\{1,q\}$. Finally $\ICG_{pq}(\{1,p,q\})$ is the
complete graph $K_{pq}$, of energy $2(pq-1)$. We have not found the maximal
energy for $n=pq$ stated explicitly in the literature, in the searches described
in Section~1. Le and Sander~\cite{lesander} study the maximum
$\tilde E_{\max}(n)$ of the energy over \emph{multiplicative} divisor sets; for
their results for general~$n$ see~\cite[Theorem~2.1 and Corollary~2.1]{lesander},
which we neither restate nor formalise. We use this only for $n=p^aq^b$: a set $\mathcal D$ is multiplicative if
$\mathcal D=\{p^iq^j:\ i\in A,\ j\in B\}$ for some $A\subseteq\{0,\dots,a\}$ and
$B\subseteq\{0,\dots,b\}$, which for a nonempty set of proper divisors means
that $\mathcal D$ is a product of a set of divisors of $p^a$ and a set of
divisors of $q^b$, and $\tilde E_{\max}(n)$ is the maximum over the
multiplicative nonempty sets of proper divisors of~$n$. These are admissible sets,
so $\tilde E_{\max}(n)\le E_{\max}(n)$, the maximum over all of them. For $n=pq$
equality holds, but for $n=pq^3$ it fails: $E_{\max}(5\cdot3^3)=584>512=\tilde
E_{\max}(5\cdot3^3)$ and $E_{\max}(3\cdot5^3)=1632>1472=\tilde
E_{\max}(3\cdot5^3)$, and in both cases the maximisers are the
non-multiplicative sets of Theorem~\ref{thm:onem}. The values $512$ and $1472$
are proved in Lean; we give no written proof of them.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:near}
Other sets come close to the maximum. Let $(a,b)=(2,2)$, $q=3$ and
$\mathcal D=\{1,3,p^2,3p^2,9p\}$, so that $Y=s_2y^{\mathsf T}$ with
$y=(1,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $Z=(Ms_2)(Ny)^{\mathsf T}$. The path of $y$ is
$z_1=-1$, $z_0=\frac13$, $z_{-1}=\frac79$, and $\NA(y)=\{0\}$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} gives $\|Ny\|_1=d_2(3)-2=23$, and
$Z_{22}=9\,\delta_2(p)\,z_{-1}>0$. By~\eqref{eq:G}, $G(Y)=23\,d_2(p)$, while
the maximum of $G$ is $25\,d_2(p)-10\,\delta_2(p)=G(Y)+4(p-3)$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:signB}). So the energy of $\mathcal D$ is smaller than
the maximal energy $62p^2-90p+40$ only by $2(p-3)$.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:onem}}\label{sec:onem}
Let $p,q,m$ and $n=pq^m$ be as in Theorem~\ref{thm:onem}; the case $n=p^mq$
follows by exchanging the names of the primes. Put $x=q$, $P=x-1$ and
$R=p-1$. As $p$ and $q$ are distinct odd primes, either $x\ge5$ and $R\ge2$,
or $x=3$ and $R\ge4$; only these inequalities are used. In both cases
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RP}
R+1<P(R-1).
\end{equation}
Let $\mu_j$ and $c_j$ be the potential of Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} for $x$,
and put $\rho=\mu_{m-1}$, $\hat c_j=c_j$ for $0\le j<m$, $\hat c_m=\rho$ and
$\hat D=\sum_{j=0}^m\hat c_j$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} and
Remark~\ref{rem:closed}, $\hat D=d_m(x)/x^m$ and $\rho=\delta_m(x)/x^m$. For
$w\in\mathbb R^{m+1}$ let $z_j(w)$, $-1\le j\le m-1$, be the
path~\eqref{eq:path} with $k=m$, and let $\nu(w)=\|T_m(x)w\|_1/x^m$. For a
sign vector $y$ put $\sigma(y)=\sum_{j\in\NA(y)}\mu_j|z_j(y)|$, so that
$\nu(y)=\hat D-2\sigma(y)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:potential}. For a statement $S$,
$[S]$ is $1$ if $S$ holds and $0$ otherwise.

For $0\le\mu\le1$ let $h_\mu$ be the function $h$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}
with $P=x-1$. Besides Lemma~\ref{lem:cell} we use three values, which follow
from the definition and from the case analysis in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}: $h_\mu(0,B)=2\mu|B|$; $h_\mu(A,B)=-2(P-\mu)|B|$ if
$AB>0$ and $|B|\le|A|$; and $h_\mu(A,B)=0$ if $AB\le0$ and $|B|\le P|A|$.

Let $\mu_\infty=P/(x+1)$. As $x\mu_\infty=P-\mu_\infty$, the recursion for
$\mu$ gives $\mu_j-\mu_\infty=(-1/x)^{j+1}(1-\mu_\infty)$, where
$1-\mu_\infty>0$. Hence, for $j\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:muinf}
\mu_j>\mu_\infty\ \text{if $j$ is odd},\qquad
\mu_j<\mu_\infty\ \text{if $j$ is even},\qquad
\mu_0\le\mu_j\le\mu_1.
\end{equation}
In particular $\rho<\mu_\infty$, because $m-1$ is even.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:realpot}
For every $w\in\mathbb R^{m+1}$,
\[
\nu(w)=\sum_{j=0}^m\hat c_j|w_j|
+\frac1x\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}h_{\mu_{j-1}}\bigl(w_j,z_j(w)\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $z_j=z_j(w)$. By~\eqref{eq:path}, $z_{j-1}-z_j=\frac Px(w_j-z_j)$ and
$xz_{j-1}=z_j+Pw_j$. Together with $x\mu_j=P-\mu_{j-1}$ this gives, for
$0\le j<m$,
\[
|z_{j-1}-z_j|+\mu_{j-1}|z_{j-1}|-\mu_j|z_j|
=\tfrac1x\bigl[h_{\mu_{j-1}}(w_j,z_j)+P(1+\mu_{j-1})|w_j|\bigr].
\]
Summing over $j$, the potential terms telescope to
$\mu_{-1}|z_{-1}|-\mu_{m-1}|z_{m-1}|=|z_{-1}|-\rho|w_m|$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:path} gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:noncorner}
For $y\in\{\pm1\}^{m+1}$ we have $\nu(y)-|z_{-1}(y)|\le\hat D-\rho$, with
equality exactly when $y=\pm s_m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:path}, the left side is $\sum_{j<m}|z_{j-1}-z_j|$, where
$z_j=z_j(y)$. Let $\mu'_{-1}=0$ and $\mu'_j=(P-\mu'_{j-1})/x$ for $j\ge0$, so
that $\mu'_j\in(0,P/x]$ for $j\ge0$. The computation in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} uses only this recursion of the potential, so it
gives
\[
|z_{j-1}-z_j|+\mu'_{j-1}|z_{j-1}|-\mu'_j|z_j|
=c'_j-2\mu'_j|z_j|\,[j\in\NA(y)],\qquad c'_j=P(1+\mu'_{j-1})/x.
\]
Summing over $j<m$ and using $\mu'_{-1}=0$ and $|z_{m-1}|=1$,
\[
\sum_{j<m}|z_{j-1}-z_j|=\sum_{j<m}c'_j+\mu'_{m-1}
-2\sum_{j\in\NA(y)}\mu'_j|z_j|.
\]
All terms of the last sum are positive, so the left side is maximal exactly
when $\NA(y)=\emptyset$, that is, when $y=\pm s_m$. For $y=s_m$ it equals
$\nu(s_m)-|z_{-1}(s_m)|=\hat D-\rho$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} and
Remark~\ref{rem:closed}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:paritydefect}
Let $y\in\{\pm1\}^{m+1}$ with $y_0=y_m$. Then $(3R-1)\,\sigma(y)>(R-1)\rho$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
As $m$ is odd and $y_0=y_m$, $y\ne\pm s_m$, so $\NA(y)$ is nonempty; let
$J=\max\NA(y)$. Since $y$ alternates on $J+1,\dots,m$, the recursion
$|z_{j-1}|=(P-|z_j|)/x$ from the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} holds for
$J<j\le m-1$, and starting from $|z_{m-1}|=1=\mu_{-1}$ it gives
$|z_J(y)|=\mu_{m-2-J}$. Hence $\sigma(y)\ge\mu_J\mu_{m-2-J}$. If $J=m-1$,
this is $\rho$, and $(3R-1)\rho>(R-1)\rho$. Otherwise $J$ and $m-2-J$ are
nonnegative and have opposite parity, since their sum is odd, so
$\sigma(y)>\mu_0\mu_\infty$ by~\eqref{eq:muinf}. As
$(3R-1)(x-2)-(R-1)x=2R(x-3)+2>0$, we have $(3R-1)\mu_0>R-1$, and therefore
$(3R-1)\sigma(y)>(3R-1)\mu_0\mu_\infty\ge(R-1)\mu_\infty\ge(R-1)\rho$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:onem}]
Let $\mathcal D$ be a set of proper divisors of $n$, and let $u$ and $v$ be
the two rows of its sign matrix $Y$, so that $v_m=-1$. Since
$T_1(p)=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-R&R\\R&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, the rows
of $Z=T_1(p)YT_m(x)^{\mathsf T}$ are $-R\,T_m(x)w^1$ and $T_m(x)w^2$, where
$w^1=u-v$ and $w^2=Ru+v$. By the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:path},
$Z_{1m}=x^mz_{-1}(w^2)$, so~\eqref{eq:G} gives $G(Y)=x^m\Phi$ with
\[
\Phi=R\,\nu(w^1)+\nu(w^2)-2\bigl(z_{-1}(w^2)\bigr)^-.
\]
As $d_1(p)=3R-1$ and $\delta_1(p)=R-1$, the claimed maximal energy is
$\frac12(n+x^m\Theta)$ with $\Theta=(3R-1)\hat D-2(R-1)\rho$. We show that
$\Phi\le\Theta$, with equality exactly for $Y=s_1s_m^{\mathsf
T}-2e_1e_m^{\mathsf T}$ and $Y=-s_1s_m^{\mathsf T}$. These are the sign
matrices of the two sets in the theorem, which are nonempty sets of proper
divisors of $n$ because $1+m$ is even; so the theorem follows.

Let $\mathcal A=\{j:\ u_j=v_j\}$. For $j\in\mathcal A$ we have $w^1_j=0$ and
$|w^2_j|=R+1$; for $j\notin\mathcal A$ we have $|w^1_j|=2$ and
$|w^2_j|=R-1$. So $R|w^1_j|+|w^2_j|$ is $R+1$ on $\mathcal A$ and $3R-1$ off
$\mathcal A$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:realpot}, applied to $w^1$ and $w^2$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi}
\Theta-\Phi=2(R-1)\Bigl(\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}\hat c_j-\rho\Bigr)-H
+2\bigl(z_{-1}(w^2)\bigr)^-,
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the sum of the $2m$ \emph{cells}
$\frac Rx\,h_{\mu_{j-1}}\bigl(w^1_j,z_j(w^1)\bigr)$ and
$\frac1x\,h_{\mu_{j-1}}\bigl(w^2_j,z_j(w^2)\bigr)$, $0\le j<m$. Recall that
$z_j(w)$ is a convex combination of $w_{j+1},\dots,w_m$.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] For the $w^2$-cells, $|z_j(w^2)|\le R+1<P(R-1)\le P|w^2_j|$
 by~\eqref{eq:RP}, so they are $\le0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}.
\item[(b)] For the $w^1$-cells with $j\notin\mathcal A$,
 $|z_j(w^1)|\le2\le P|w^1_j|$, so they are $\le0$.
\item[(c)] A $w^1$-cell with $j\in\mathcal A$ equals
 $\frac Rx h_{\mu_{j-1}}(0,z_j(w^1))=\frac{2R}x\mu_{j-1}|z_j(w^1)|$.
\end{itemize}
For $j\in\mathcal A$ with $j<m$ let
\[
s_j=2(R-1)c_j-\tfrac{2R}x\,\mu_{j-1}|z_j(w^1)|
\ \ge\ \tfrac2x\bigl[(R-1)P(1+\mu)-2R\mu\bigr],\qquad \mu=\mu_{j-1},
\]
where we used $|z_j(w^1)|\le2$. The bracket is affine in $\mu$, and it equals
$(R-1)P>0$ at $\mu=0$ and $2[R(P-1)-P]>0$ at $\mu=1$; hence $s_j>0$. Let
$S=\sum_{j\in\mathcal A,\,j<m}s_j$, let $C\ge0$ be minus the sum of the cells
in (a) and~(b), and let $K=2(z_{-1}(w^2))^-\ge0$. Then~\eqref{eq:Phi} reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget}
\Theta-\Phi=S+C+K-2(R-1)\rho\,[m\notin\mathcal A].
\end{equation}
Moreover $|z_0(w^2)|\le R+1<P|w^2_0|$, so $z_{-1}(w^2)=(z_0(w^2)+Pw^2_0)/x$
has the sign of $w^2_0=Ru_0+v_0$, which is the sign of $u_0$. Thus $K=0$ if
and only if $u_0=1$.

\emph{Case 1: $m\in\mathcal A$.} Then $\Theta-\Phi=S+C+K\ge0$. Suppose that
equality holds. Then $S=0$, so $\mathcal A=\{m\}$; $K=0$, so $u_0=1$; and
$C=0$. Now $w^1_j=2u_j$ for $j<m$ and $w^1_m=0$. For $j\le m-2$ we have
$|Pw^1_{j+1}|=2P>2\ge|z_{j+1}(w^1)|$, so
$z_j(w^1)=(z_{j+1}(w^1)+Pw^1_{j+1})/x$ has the sign of $u_{j+1}$, and
$|z_j(w^1)|\le2=|w^1_j|$. If $u_j=u_{j+1}$ for some $j\le m-2$, the
$w^1$-cell at $j$ is therefore $-\frac{2R}x(P-\mu_{j-1})|z_j(w^1)|<0$,
contradicting $C=0$. So $u$ alternates on $0,\dots,m-1$ and $u_0=1$; together
with $u_m=v_m=-1$ and $v_j=-u_j$ for $j<m$, this means
$Y=s_1s_m^{\mathsf T}-2e_1e_m^{\mathsf T}$, as $m$ is odd. Conversely, for
this $Y$ we have $\mathcal A=\{m\}$, $u=s_m$ and $S=K=0$. The $w^1$-cells
vanish: for $j\le m-2$, $w^1_j=2u_j$ and $z_j(w^1)$ have opposite signs and
$|z_j(w^1)|\le P|w^1_j|$, and $z_{m-1}(w^1)=w^1_m=0$. The $w^2$-cells vanish
too: $w^2_i$ has the sign of $u_i$ for every $i$; by~\eqref{eq:RP},
$z_j(w^2)=(z_{j+1}(w^2)+Pw^2_{j+1})/x$ has the sign of $w^2_{j+1}$ for
$j\le m-2$, and $z_{m-1}(w^2)=w^2_m$; since $u$ alternates, $w^2_j$ and
$z_j(w^2)$ have opposite signs, and $|z_j(w^2)|\le P|w^2_j|$ by~(a). Hence
$\Phi=\Theta$.

\emph{Case 2: $m\notin\mathcal A\ne\emptyset$.} Then $u_m=1$ and
$\mathcal A\subseteq\{0,\dots,m-1\}$. By~\eqref{eq:budget} it suffices to
find $k\in\mathcal A$ with $s_k>2(R-1)\rho$. Since
$\rho<\mu_\infty=P/(P+2)$, the lower bound for $s_k$ shows that this holds
if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:case2}
(R-1)P+\mu(P+2)\bigl[R(P-2)-P\bigr]\ge0,\qquad \mu=\mu_{k-1}.
\end{equation}
If $x\ge5$, then $P\ge4$ and $R(P-2)-P\ge P-4\ge0$, so~\eqref{eq:case2}
holds for every $k\in\mathcal A$. If $x=3$, then~\eqref{eq:case2} reads
$2(R-1)\ge8\mu$. If some $k\in\mathcal A$ has $k\ge1$, then
$\mu_{k-1}\le\mu_1=\frac59$ by~\eqref{eq:muinf}, and
$2(R-1)\ge6>\frac{40}9$. It remains to treat $x=3$ and $\mathcal A=\{0\}$.
Here $\mu_\infty=\frac12$ and $c_0=\frac43$, and since $z_0(w)$ depends only
on $w_1,\dots,w_m$, we have $z_0(w^1)=2z_0(u)$ and
$s_0=\frac23\bigl[4(R-1)-2R|z_0(u)|\bigr]$. If $m=1$, then $|z_0(u)|=1$,
$\rho=\mu_0=\frac13$ and $s_0=\frac23(2R-4)>\frac23(R-1)=2(R-1)\rho$. Let
$m\ge3$. If $|z_0(u)|\le\frac{5(R-1)}{4R}$, then $s_0\ge R-1>2(R-1)\rho$.
Otherwise $|z_0(u)|>\frac{15}{16}$. If $u_1\ne u_2$, then
$u_1=-\operatorname{sgn}z_1(u)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:potential}, and
$|z_0(u)|=(2-|z_1(u)|)/3\le\frac59$; hence $u_1=u_2$. Then
$1\notin\mathcal A$, and the arguments $w^1_1=2u_1$ and $z_1(w^1)=2z_1(u)$ of
the $w^1$-cell at $j=1$ have the same sign, with $|z_1(w^1)|\le|w^1_1|$. This
cell equals
$-\frac{2R}3(P-\mu_0)|z_1(w^1)|=-\frac{20R}9|z_1(u)|\le-\frac{20R}{27}$, so
$C\ge\frac{20R}{27}$. With $s_0\ge\frac23(2R-4)$ and $2(R-1)\rho<R-1$,
\[
\Theta-\Phi\ge\tfrac23(2R-4)+\tfrac{20R}{27}-2(R-1)\rho
>\tfrac{56R-72}{27}-(R-1)=\tfrac{29R-45}{27}>0.
\]

\emph{Case 3: $\mathcal A=\emptyset$}, that is, $v=-u$. Then $u_m=1$,
$w^1=2u$, $w^2=(R-1)u$ and $\Phi=(3R-1)\nu(u)-2(R-1)(z_{-1}(u))^-$. If
$u_0=1$, then $z_{-1}(u)>0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:potential}, and
$\Phi=(3R-1)(\hat D-2\sigma(u))<\Theta$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:paritydefect},
since $u_0=u_m$. If $u_0=-1$, then $(z_{-1}(u))^-=|z_{-1}(u)|$ and
\[
\Phi=(R+1)\,\nu(u)+2(R-1)\bigl(\nu(u)-|z_{-1}(u)|\bigr)
\le(R+1)\hat D+2(R-1)(\hat D-\rho)=\Theta
\]
by Lemmas~\ref{lem:potential} and~\ref{lem:noncorner}, with equality exactly
when $u=\pm s_m$, that is, $u=-s_m$. This is $Y=-s_1s_m^{\mathsf T}$.

The three cases cover all $Y$, which proves the theorem. For $m=1$ the value
is $4(p-1)(q-1)$, since $d_1(q)=3q-4$ and $\delta_1(q)=q-2$.
\end{proof}

\section{The column path}\label{sec:column}
In this section and the next two, the exponent $a\ge2$ of $p$ is fixed, $b\ge1$
is any integer with $a+b$ even, and $p,q$ are real numbers with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regions}
p\ge5\ \text{and}\ q\ge3,\qquad\text{or}\qquad p=3\ \text{and}\ q\ge5.
\end{equation}
Every ordered pair of distinct odd primes satisfies~\eqref{eq:regions}. We use
the notation of Section~\ref{sec:onem} with $m$ replaced by $b$: $x=q$,
$P=x-1$ and $R=p-1$; $\mu_j$ and $c_j$ form the potential of
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} for $x$, and $\mu_\infty=P/(x+1)$; $\rho=\mu_{b-1}$,
$\hat c_j=c_j$ for $j<b$, $\hat c_b=\rho$ and $\hat D=\sum_{j=0}^b\hat c_j$,
so that $\hat D=d_b(q)/x^b$ and $\rho=\delta_b(q)/x^b$; for
$w\in\mathbb R^{b+1}$, $z_j(w)$ is the path~\eqref{eq:path} with $k=b$ and
$\nu(w)=\|T_b(x)w\|_1/x^b$; and $h_\mu$ is the function $h$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:cell} with $P=x-1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:realpot}
and~\eqref{eq:muinf} hold with the same proofs, which do not use that $m$ is
odd. By Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} and~\eqref{eq:muinf},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:murange}
\mu_0\le\mu_j\le1\ \ (j\ge-1),\qquad \mu_j\le\mu_1\ \ (j\ge0),\qquad
\mu_0=\frac{P-1}{x},\quad \mu_1=\frac{P^2+1}{x^2}.
\end{equation}
By~\eqref{eq:regions}, either $R\ge4$ and $P\ge2$, or $R=2$ and $P\ge4$; in
particular $P\ge2$ and $PR\ge8$.

\paragraph{Values of the cell function.} Besides the three values of $h_\mu$
listed in Section~\ref{sec:onem}, the case analysis in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}, together with $h_\mu(-A,-B)=h_\mu(A,B)$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hmore}
h_\mu(A,B)=\begin{cases}2(\mu|B|-P|A|)&\text{if $AB>0$ and $|B|\ge|A|$,}\\
2\mu(|B|-P|A|)&\text{if $AB\le0$ and $|B|\ge P|A|$.}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $0\le\mu\le1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hneg}
h_\mu(A,B)<0\qquad\text{if $AB>0$ and $|B|<P|A|$},
\end{equation}
since the value is then $-2(P-\mu)|B|$ or $2(\mu|B|-P|A|)\le2(|B|-P|A|)$. The
assumption $|B|<P|A|$ is needed here: $h_\mu(A,PA/\mu)=0$ for $A>0$ and
$0<\mu\le1$.

\paragraph{The matrix $M$.} Let $M=T_a(p)$, $\alpha=Ms_a$ and $u=Me_a$, and
let $\omega_i$ be the sum of the absolute values of the entries of row $i$ of
$M$. Thus $\|\alpha\|_1=d_a(p)$, $\alpha_a=\delta_a(p)$ by
Remark~\ref{rem:closed}, and $|(M\zeta)_i|\le\omega_i$ for
$\zeta\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$. Let $\mu^{(p)}_j$ denote the potential of
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} for $p$ in place of $x$. For $i<a$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mfacts}
u_i=p^{a-1-i}R,\qquad \omega_i=2p^{a-1}R,\qquad
\alpha_i=(-1)^{a-i}p^{a-1}R\bigl(1+\mu^{(p)}_{i-1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and $u_a=1$, $\omega_a=p^a$. Indeed, by the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:path}
with $x=p$ and $k=a$, row $i<a$ of $M$ has the entry $-p^{a-1}R$ in column
$l=a-1-i$ and nonnegative entries with sum $p^{a-1}R$ in the columns after
$l$, and $\alpha_i=p^{a-1}R\,(z_l-(s_a)_l)$ for the path $z$ of $s_a$, where
$\operatorname{sgn}z_l=(s_a)_{l+1}$ and $|z_l|=\mu^{(p)}_{a-2-l}$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} and Remark~\ref{rem:closed}; the last row of $M$ is
$(p^{a-1}R,\dots,pR,R,1)$. Moreover
$\delta_a(p)=p^a\mu^{(p)}_{a-1}\ge p^{a-1}(p-2)\ge3$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Afacts}
2u_i\le|\alpha_i|\ \ (i<a),\qquad \delta_a(p)\ge3,\qquad
P\,\delta_a(p)>p^a,\qquad \omega_i\le P|\alpha_i|\ \ (0\le i\le a).
\end{equation}
The first inequality reads $2\le p^i(1+\mu^{(p)}_{i-1})$, with equality for
$i=0$. Next, $P\delta_a(p)\ge2p^{a-1}(p-2)>p^a$ if $p\ge5$, and
$P\delta_a(p)\ge4\cdot3^{a-1}>3^a$ if $p=3$. Finally
$\omega_i\le2|\alpha_i|\le P|\alpha_i|$ for $i<a$, and
$\omega_a=p^a<P\delta_a(p)$.

\paragraph{Columns.} For $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ put $\Delta(c)=d_a(p)-\|Mc\|_1$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:potential}, $\Delta(c)\ge0$, with equality exactly for
$c=\pm s_a$. Let $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}$ have columns
$c_0,\dots,c_b$. Define $\zeta_{b-1}=c_b$ and $\zeta_{j-1}=(\zeta_j+Pc_j)/x$
for $j=b-1,\dots,0$; this is the path~\eqref{eq:path} applied to the sequence
of columns. Each $\zeta_j$ is a convex combination of $c_{j+1},\dots,c_b$, so
$\zeta_j\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$, and if $r_0,\dots,r_a$ are the rows of $MY$, then
$z_j(r_i)=(M\zeta_j)_i$ by linearity. For $0\le\mu\le1$,
$c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $\zeta\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$ let
\[
\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)=P(1+\mu)\,\Delta(c)-\sum_{i=0}^{a}
h_\mu\bigl((Mc)_i,(M\zeta)_i\bigr);
\]
we call the terms $h_\mu((Mc)_i,(M\zeta)_i)$ the \emph{cells} of
$\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)$. As $h_\mu(-A,-B)=h_\mu(A,B)$, we have
$\psi_\mu(-c,-\zeta)=\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)$. For $0\le j<b$ put
$\psi_j=\psi_{\mu_{j-1}}(c_j,\zeta_j)$.

Let $N=T_b(q)$ and $\Theta=d_a(p)\,d_b(q)-2\delta_a(p)\,\delta_b(q)$, and let
$G(Y)$ be the middle expression in~\eqref{eq:G} with $Z=MYN^{\mathsf T}$. Let
$Y^-=-s_as_b^{\mathsf T}$ and $Y^+=s_as_b^{\mathsf T}-2e_ae_b^{\mathsf T}$.
The columns of $Y^-$ are $(-1)^{j+1}s_a$; those of $Y^+$ are $(-1)^js_a$ for
$j<b$ and $g_+=(-1)^as_a-2e_a$ for $j=b$, as $(-1)^b=(-1)^a$. By
Section~\ref{sec:equal}, $G(Y^-)=\Theta$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:colid}
For every $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}$,
\[
x^{1-b}\bigl(\Theta-G(Y)\bigr)=\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\psi_j
+x\rho\bigl(\Delta(c_b)-2\delta_a(p)\bigr)+2x\bigl((M\zeta_{-1})_a\bigr)^-.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Row $i$ of $Z$ is $(Nr_i)^{\mathsf T}$, so the absolute values of its entries
have sum $x^b\nu(r_i)$, and $Z_{ab}=(Nr_a)_b=x^bz_{-1}(r_a)=x^b(M\zeta_{-1})_a$
by the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:path}. Hence
$G(Y)=x^b\bigl[\sum_i\nu(r_i)-2((M\zeta_{-1})_a)^-\bigr]$ by~\eqref{eq:G}. We
apply Lemma~\ref{lem:realpot} to each $r_i$ and add. As
$\sum_i|(r_i)_j|=\|Mc_j\|_1=d_a(p)-\Delta(c_j)$, $z_j(r_i)=(M\zeta_j)_i$ and
$\hat c_j=P(1+\mu_{j-1})/x$ for $j<b$, this gives
\[
x^{-b}G(Y)=d_a(p)\hat D-\sum_{j=0}^{b}\hat c_j\Delta(c_j)
+\frac1x\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}\sum_{i=0}^{a}h_{\mu_{j-1}}\bigl((Mc_j)_i,(M\zeta_j)_i\bigr)
-2\bigl((M\zeta_{-1})_a\bigr)^-.
\]
Since $x^{-b}\Theta=d_a(p)\hat D-2\delta_a(p)\rho$ and $\hat c_b=\rho$,
subtracting and multiplying by $x$ proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:onecol}
Let $0\le\mu\le1$, $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $\zeta\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$. Then
\[
\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)\ge P(1+\mu)\Delta(c)-2\mu\sum_{i=0}^a
\max\bigl\{0,\ |(M\zeta)_i|-P|(Mc)_i|\bigr\}.
\]
If $c=\pm s_a$, all cells of $\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)$ are $\le0$; so
$\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)\ge0$, with equality exactly when all cells vanish.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The bound follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}, which applies as $P\ge2>\mu$. If
$c=\pm s_a$, then $\Delta(c)=0$ and
$|(M\zeta)_i|\le\omega_i\le P|\alpha_i|=P|(Mc)_i|$ by~\eqref{eq:Afacts}, so
every cell is $\le0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:chain}
Let $g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $0\le k\le b$, and suppose that $c_b=g$ and
$c_{b-i}=(-1)^ig$ for $1\le i\le k$. Then $\zeta_{b-1-i}=(-1)^i\mu_{i-1}g$
for $0\le i\le k$, and $\psi_{b-i}=P(1+\mu_{b-i-1})\Delta(g)$ for
$1\le i\le k$. In particular, if $c_l=(-1)^{l+1}s_a$ for $J<l\le b$, then
$\zeta_J=(-1)^J\mu_{b-2-J}s_a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The formula for $\zeta_{b-1-i}$ holds for $i=0$ as $\mu_{-1}=1$, and if it
holds for $i-1$, then
$\zeta_{b-1-i}=\bigl((-1)^{i-1}\mu_{i-2}g+P(-1)^ig\bigr)/x=(-1)^i\mu_{i-1}g$.
For $1\le i\le k$, the cells of $\psi_{b-i}$ have the arguments
$A=(-1)^iMg$ and $B=M\zeta_{b-i}=-\mu_{i-2}A$, so that $A_lB_l\le0$ and
$|B_l|\le|A_l|\le P|A_l|$ for every $l$, and all cells vanish. For the last
claim take $g=(-1)^{b+1}s_a$ and $i=b-1-J$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:eqgen}
We have $\Delta(g_+)=2\delta_a(p)$. For $Y=Y^+$, every $\psi_j$ vanishes and
$(M\zeta_{-1})_a>0$, so that $G(Y^+)=\Theta$. Conversely, if $c_b=g_+$ and,
for every $j<b$, $c_j=\pm s_a$ and $\psi_j=0$, then $Y=Y^+$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:Mfacts},
$(Mg_+)_i=(-1)^a\alpha_i-2u_i=(-1)^i\bigl(|\alpha_i|-2(-1)^iu_i\bigr)$, and
$|\alpha_i|-2(-1)^iu_i\ge0$ by~\eqref{eq:Afacts}. Hence
$\|Mg_+\|_1=\sum_i|\alpha_i|-2\sum_i(-1)^iu_i=d_a(p)-2s_a^{\mathsf T}Me_a
=d_a(p)-2\delta_a(p)$, as $M$ is symmetric.

Let $Y=Y^+$. By induction on $j$ downwards,
$\zeta_j=(-1)^{j+1}\mu_{b-2-j}s_a-2x^{j+1-b}e_a$ for $-1\le j\le b-1$. At a
column $j<b$, the cells of $\psi_j$ have the arguments $A_i=(-1)^j\alpha_i$
and $B_i=(M\zeta_j)_i$, and with $\sigma_i=\operatorname{sgn}A_i=(-1)^{j+a-i}$,
\[
\sigma_iB_i=-\mu_{b-2-j}|\alpha_i|-2(-1)^{j+a-i}x^{j+1-b}u_i.
\]
We claim that $-P|A_i|\le\sigma_iB_i\le0$, so that every cell vanishes. The
upper bound holds if $\mu_{b-2-j}|\alpha_i|\ge2x^{j+1-b}u_i$: for $j=b-1$ this
is $|\alpha_i|\ge2u_i$, and for $j\le b-2$ it follows from
$\mu_{b-2-j}\ge\mu_0=(P-1)/x$, $x^{j+1-b}\le1/x$ and
$(P-1)|\alpha_i|\ge|\alpha_i|\ge2u_i$, where $|\alpha_a|=\delta_a(p)$ and
$u_a=1$. The lower bound holds as
$\mu_{b-2-j}|\alpha_i|+2u_i\le2|\alpha_i|\le P|\alpha_i|$. Since
$\Delta(\pm s_a)=0$, every $\psi_j$ vanishes. Moreover
$(M\zeta_{-1})_a=\mu_{b-1}\delta_a(p)-2x^{-b}\ge\bigl((P-1)\delta_a(p)-2\bigr)/x>0$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:colid} and $\Delta(g_+)=2\delta_a(p)$, $G(Y^+)=\Theta$.

Conversely, let $c_b=g_+$, and let $c_j=\varepsilon_js_a$ with
$\varepsilon_j=\pm1$ and $\psi_j=0$ for $j<b$; by Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol}, all
cells of every $\psi_j$ vanish. Put $\beta_j=(M\zeta_j)_a$, so that
$|\beta_j|\le\omega_a=p^a$. We show $\varepsilon_j=(-1)^j$ by induction on $j$
downwards. The cell of $\psi_j$ at $i=a$ has the arguments
$A=\varepsilon_j\delta_a(p)$ and $B=\beta_j$, and by~\eqref{eq:hneg} it is
negative if $AB>0$ and $|B|<P|A|$. So it suffices to check these two
conditions for $\varepsilon_j=(-1)^{j+1}$. For $j=b-1$,
$\beta_{b-1}=(Mg_+)_a=(-1)^b\delta_a(p)-2$ has the sign $(-1)^b$, and
$|\beta_{b-1}|\le\delta_a(p)+2<P\delta_a(p)$. For $j\le b-2$, if
$\varepsilon_{j+1}=(-1)^{j+1}$, then
$\beta_j=\bigl(\beta_{j+1}+(-1)^{j+1}P\delta_a(p)\bigr)/x$ has the sign
$(-1)^{j+1}$, since $|\beta_{j+1}|\le p^a<P\delta_a(p)$, and
$|\beta_j|\le p^a<P\delta_a(p)$. Hence $\varepsilon_j=(-1)^j$ for all $j<b$,
that is, $Y=Y^+$.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ptwo}}\label{sec:ptwo}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:signB}
Let $b\ge2$ be even, let $p,q$ be real numbers with $p\ge5$ and $q\ge3$, or
with $p=3$ and $q\ge5$, and put
$\Theta=d_2(p)\,d_b(q)-2\delta_2(p)\,\delta_b(q)$. Every
$Y\in\{\pm1\}^{3\times(b+1)}$ with $y_{2b}=-1$ satisfies $G(Y)\le\Theta$,
where $G(Y)$ is the middle expression in~\eqref{eq:G} with
$Z=T_2(p)\,Y\,T_b(q)^{\mathsf T}$, and equality holds exactly for
$Y=-s_2s_b^{\mathsf T}$ and $Y=s_2s_b^{\mathsf T}-2e_2e_b^{\mathsf T}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ptwo}]
Let $n=p^2q^b$. By~\eqref{eq:G}, $2\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D))=n+G(Y)$ for the sign
matrix $Y$ of a set $\mathcal D$ of proper divisors of $n$, and $y_{2b}=-1$.
The two matrices in Theorem~\ref{thm:signB} are the sign matrices of
$\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{odd}\}$ and of
$\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{even}\}\setminus\{n\}$. These are nonempty sets of
proper divisors of $n$: the first contains $p$, and it does not contain $n$
because $2+b$ is even, and the second contains~$1$. Distinct odd primes
satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:signB}, so the maximal energy is
$\frac12(n+\Theta)$, attained exactly by these two sets. By
Remark~\ref{rem:closed}, $d_2(p)=5p^2-8p+4$ and $\delta_2(p)=p^2-2p+2$. The
case $n=p^bq^2$ follows by exchanging the names of the primes.
\end{proof}

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm:signB}, with the notation of
Section~\ref{sec:column} for $a=2$. Here
\[
M=T_2(p)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-pR&pR\\-pR&R^2&R\\pR&R&1\end{pmatrix},
\]
and we put $g_1=s_2=(1,-1,1)^{\mathsf T}$, $g_2=(1,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$,
$g_3=(1,-1,-1)^{\mathsf T}=g_+$ and $g_4=(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}$. Using $p=R+1$,
\[
Mg_1=\begin{pmatrix}2pR\\-2R^2\\R^2+1\end{pmatrix},\quad
Mg_2=\begin{pmatrix}-2pR\\-2R\\R^2+2R-1\end{pmatrix},\quad
Mg_3=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-2pR\\R^2-1\end{pmatrix},\quad
Mg_4=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\p^2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Every $c\in\{\pm1\}^3$ equals $\pm g_k$ for exactly one $k$. As
$d_2(p)=5R^2+2R+1$ and $\delta_2(p)=R^2+1$, we get $\Delta(\pm g_k)=\Delta_k$
with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Deltas}
\Delta_1=0,\qquad\Delta_2=2(R-1)^2,\qquad\Delta_3=2(R^2+1)=2\delta_2(p),
\qquad\Delta_4=4R^2.
\end{equation}
Moreover $\omega=(2pR,2pR,p^2)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Wtwo}
p^2<P(R^2+2R-1),\qquad p^2<P(R^2-1),
\end{equation}
because $2(R^2+2R-1)-p^2=(R+3)(R-1)>0$, $2(R^2-1)-p^2=(R+1)(R-3)>0$ if
$R\ge4$, and $P(R^2-1)\ge12>9=p^2$ if $R=2$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:colS}
Let $0\le\mu\le1$ and $\zeta\in[-1,1]^3$, and put $B=M\zeta$. If
$c\ne\pm s_2$, then $\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)>0$. Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S34}
\psi_\mu(\pm g_3,\zeta)\ge2P(1+\mu)(R^2+1)-2\mu|B_0|,\qquad
\psi_\mu(\pm g_4,\zeta)\ge4PR^2(1+\mu)-2\mu\bigl(|B_0|+|B_1|\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We use Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol}, $|B_i|\le\omega_i$ and~\eqref{eq:Wtwo}. For
$c=\pm g_2$ we have $|Mc|=(2pR,2R,R^2+2R-1)$, so the terms with $i=0,2$ of
the sum in Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol} vanish, and
$\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)\ge2P(1+\mu)(R-1)^2-4\mu R\max\{0,p-P\}$. If $P\ge p$, this
is positive. Otherwise $R\ge4$ by~\eqref{eq:regions}, and the bound is affine
in $\mu$, positive at $\mu=0$, and at $\mu=1$ equal to
$4[P(R^2-R+1)-R(R+1)]\ge4(R-1)(R-2)>0$. For $c=\pm g_3$ we have
$|Mc|=(0,2pR,R^2-1)$, so the terms with $i=1,2$ vanish, which gives the first
bound in~\eqref{eq:S34}; with $|B_0|\le2pR$ and $P\ge2$ its right side is at
least $4(1+\mu)(R^2+1)-4\mu pR=4(R^2+1)-4\mu(R-1)>0$. For $c=\pm g_4$ we have
$|Mc|=(0,0,p^2)$, which gives the second bound; its right side is at least
$8R^2(1+\mu)-8\mu pR=8R(R-\mu)>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ineq}
Let $R\ge4$ and $P\ge2$, or $R=2$ and $P\ge4$, and put
$T=(R^2+1)(P^2+1)$. Then:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] $(P-1)^2(5R^2+2R+1)>T$;
\item[(ii)] $P(P+1)(R-1)^2+(P-1)^2(3R^2+1)>T$;
\item[(iii)] $(R^2+1)(P-1)+(P+1)\bigl[2P(R^2-R+1)-3R^2-1\bigr]>0$;
\item[(iv)] $P(P+1)(R-1)^2+2(P-1)^2R(R+1)>T$;
\item[(v)] $(P+1)\bigl[P(R-1)^2+2P^2(R+1)\bigr]>T$;
\item[(vi)] $(P+1)\bigl[2PR(R-3)+2P^2(R+1)\bigr]>T$ if $R\ge4$;
\item[(vii)] $P(P+1)^2(R^2+1)>2R(R+1)(P^2+1)$;
\item[(viii)] $2R(PR-1)(P+1)>T$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The function $f(P)=(P-1)^2/(P^2+1)=1-2P/(P^2+1)$ increases for $P\ge1$, and
$f(2)=\frac15$, $f(4)=\frac9{17}$.
(i) We have $5R^2+2R+1-5(R^2+1)=2(R-2)\ge0$ and
$5(P-1)^2-(P^2+1)=2(2P-1)(P-2)\ge0$, so
$(P-1)^2(5R^2+2R+1)\ge5(P-1)^2(R^2+1)\ge T$, and one of the two inequalities
is strict.
(ii) The left side minus $T$ equals
$(R-1)^2(P-1)+(P-1)^2(3R^2+1)-2R(P^2+1)$, and
$(P-1)^2(3R^2+1)=f(P)(3R^2+1)(P^2+1)\ge2R(P^2+1)$: if $R\ge4$, then
$f(P)\ge\frac15$ and $3R^2+1-10R=R(3R-10)+1>0$; if $R=2$, then
$f(P)\ge\frac9{17}$ and $\frac9{17}\cdot13>4$.
(iii) If $R\ge4$, then
$2P(R^2-R+1)-3R^2-1\ge4(R^2-R+1)-3R^2-1=(R-1)(R-3)>0$; if $R=2$, it equals
$6P-13>0$.
(iv) The left side minus $T$ equals
$(R-1)^2(P-1)+2R\bigl[(P-1)^2(R+1)-(P^2+1)\bigr]$, and $f(P)(R+1)\ge1$ both
for $R\ge4$ and for $R=2$.
(v) The left side minus $T$ equals
$(R-1)^2(P-1)+2\bigl[R(P^3-1)+P^3+P^2\bigr]>0$.
(vi) As $P(P+1)\ge P^2+1$, $R\ge3$, $P^2\ge\frac45(P^2+1)$ and $P+1\ge3$, the
left side is at least $(P^2+1)\bigl[2R(R-3)+\frac{24}5(R+1)\bigr]$, and
$2R(R-3)+\frac{24}5(R+1)-(R^2+1)=R^2-\frac65R+\frac{19}5>0$.
(vii) $P(P+1)^2=(P+2)(P^2+1)-2$, so $P(P+1)^2\ge\frac{18}5(P^2+1)$ for $P\ge2$,
and $\frac{18}5(R^2+1)-2R(R+1)=\frac15(8R^2-10R+18)>0$.
(viii) Twice the left side minus $2T$ equals
$PR(PR-4)+P^2(R^2-2)+(4PR^2-2R^2-4R-2)$, where $PR\ge8$, $R\ge2$ and
$4PR^2-2R^2-4R-2\ge2(3R+1)(R-1)>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fd1}
Let $b$ be even, $0\le J<b$, $\mu=\mu_{J-1}$ and $m=\mu_{b-2-J}$. For every
$c\in\{\pm1\}^3$ other than $-s_2$, $\psi_\mu(c,ms_2)>2x\delta_2(p)\rho$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:murange}, $\mu,m\in[\mu_0,1]$ and $\rho\le\mu_1=(P^2+1)/x^2$.
Put $T=(R^2+1)(P^2+1)$ and $\psi=\psi_\mu(c,ms_2)$. Then
$2x\delta_2(p)\rho\le2T/x$, so it suffices to show $x\psi>2T$; we do so
except in one subcase of~(d). We use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K}
x(P-\mu)m\ge(P-1)^2,
\end{equation}
which holds as $P-\mu\ge P-1\ge0$ and $xm\ge x\mu_0=P-1$. Let $A=Mc$ and
$B=M(ms_2)=m\,(2pR,-2R^2,R^2+1)$, and write $h_i=h_\mu(A_i,B_i)$; the cells
are computed with the values of $h_\mu$ in Section~\ref{sec:onem}
and~\eqref{eq:hmore}, using $0\le m\le1$.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] $c=s_2$. For each $i$, $A_iB_i\ge0$ and $|B_i|\le|A_i|$, so
 $h_i=-2(P-\mu)m|A_i|$ and $\psi=2(P-\mu)m\,d_2(p)$. By~\eqref{eq:K} and
 Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(i), $x\psi\ge2(P-1)^2(5R^2+2R+1)>2T$.
\item[(b)] $c=g_2$, so $A=(-2pR,-2R,R^2+2R-1)$. Here $h_0=0$, as
 $A_0B_0\le0$ and $|B_0|\le P|A_0|$, and $h_2=-2(P-\mu)m(R^2+1)$, as
 $A_2B_2\ge0$ and $|B_2|\le|A_2|$; also $A_1B_1\ge0$. If $mR\le1$, then
 $|B_1|\le|A_1|$, $h_1=-4(P-\mu)mR^2$ and
 $\psi=2P(1+\mu)(R-1)^2+2(P-\mu)m(3R^2+1)$, so $x\psi>2T$ by $\mu\ge0$,
 \eqref{eq:K} and Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(ii). If $mR>1$, then
 $h_1=4R(\mu mR-P)$ by~\eqref{eq:hmore}, and expanding gives
 \[
 x\psi-2T=2(R^2+1)(P-1)+2Px(R-1)^2\mu+2Px(R^2+1)m-2x(3R^2+1)\mu m.
 \]
 This is affine in $\mu$ and in $m$ separately, so its minimum over
 $[0,1]^2$ is attained at a corner. It is positive at the corners with
 $\mu m=0$, and at $\mu=m=1$ it equals twice the left side of
 Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(iii).
\item[(c)] $c=-g_2$, so $A=(2pR,2R,-(R^2+2R-1))$. Here
 $h_0=-4(P-\mu)mpR$ and $h_2=0$, and $A_1B_1\le0$. If $mR\le P$, then
 $|B_1|\le P|A_1|$, $h_1=0$ and $\psi=2P(1+\mu)(R-1)^2+4(P-\mu)mpR$, so
 $x\psi>2T$ by~\eqref{eq:K} and Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(iv). If $mR>P$, then
 $R\ge4$, since $mR\le2<P$ when $R=2$; now $h_1=4\mu R(mR-P)$
 by~\eqref{eq:hmore}, and
 \[
 \psi=2P(1+\mu)(R-1)^2+4\mu PR+4mR\bigl(Pp-\mu(2R+1)\bigr).
 \]
 As $Pp-\mu(2R+1)\ge2(R+1)-(2R+1)>0$ and $mR>P$, we get
 $\psi>2P(R-1)^2+4P^2(R+1)+2\mu P(R^2-4R-1)$. The right side is affine in
 $\mu$, and $x$ times it exceeds $2T$ at $\mu=0$ by
 Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(v) and at $\mu=1$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(vi), since
 $(R-1)^2+R^2-4R-1=2R(R-3)$.
\item[(d)] $c=\pm g_3$. By~\eqref{eq:S34} with $|B_0|=2mpR$,
 $\psi\ge2P(1+\mu)(R^2+1)-4\mu mpR$. If $J\le b-2$, then $m\le\mu_1$
 by~\eqref{eq:murange}, so $2mpR\le2pR(P^2+1)/x^2<P(R^2+1)$ by
 Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(vii); hence $\psi\ge2P(R^2+1)$ and
 $x\psi\ge2P(P+1)(R^2+1)>2T$. If $J=b-1$, then $m=\mu_{-1}=1$,
 $\mu=\mu_{b-2}>0$ and $x\rho=P-\mu$, and directly
 \[
 \psi-2x\delta_2(p)\rho\ge2\mu\bigl[(R^2+1)x-2pR\bigr]\ge2\mu(R^2-2R+3)>0.
 \]
\item[(e)] $c=\pm g_4$. By~\eqref{eq:S34} with $|B_0|+|B_1|=2mR(2R+1)$ and
 $m\le1$,
 $\psi\ge4PR^2(1+\mu)-4\mu R(2R+1)=4R(PR-1)+4R\bigl[\mu R(P-2)+1-\mu\bigr]
 \ge4R(PR-1)$, so $x\psi>2T$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:ineq}(viii).
\end{itemize}
These are all the columns $c\ne-s_2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fd2}
Let $b$ be even. If $c_b=g_2$, then $\sum_{j<b}\psi_j>4Rx\rho$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All $\psi_j$ are nonnegative by Lemmas~\ref{lem:onecol} and~\ref{lem:colS}.
Here $\zeta_{b-1}=g_2$, so $B=M\zeta_{b-1}=(-2pR,-2R,R^2+2R-1)$, and
$x\rho=P-\mu$ with $\mu=\mu_{b-2}$. Let $c=c_{b-1}$, $A=Mc$ and
$h_i=h_\mu(A_i,B_i)$.

Suppose first that $c\ne-g_2$ or $R\ge4$. We show that
$\psi_{b-1}>4R(P-\mu)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $c=\pm g_4$: by~\eqref{eq:S34},
 $\psi_{b-1}-4R(P-\mu)\ge4PR^2(1+\mu)-4\mu R(R+2)-4R(P-\mu)
 =4PR(R-1)+4\mu R(PR-R-1)>0$.
\item $c=\pm g_3$: by~\eqref{eq:S34},
 $\psi_{b-1}-4R(P-\mu)\ge2P(1+\mu)(R^2+1)-4\mu pR-4R(P-\mu)
 =2P(R-1)^2+2\mu\bigl[P(R^2+1)-2R^2\bigr]>0$.
\item $c=g_2$ or $c=-s_2$: then $A_0=B_0<0$, so $h_0=-4(P-\mu)pR$, while
 $h_1,h_2\le0$, for $g_2$ because $A_i=B_i$ and for $-s_2$ by
 Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol}. Hence $\psi_{b-1}\ge4(P-\mu)pR>4R(P-\mu)$.
\item $c=s_2$: here $A=(2pR,-2R^2,R^2+1)$, so $h_0=0$, $h_1=-4(P-\mu)R$ and
 $h_2=2\mu(R^2+2R-1)-2P(R^2+1)$ by~\eqref{eq:hmore}. Hence
 $\psi_{b-1}-4R(P-\mu)=2P(R^2+1)-2\mu(R^2+2R-1)\ge2(R^2-2R+3)>0$.
\item $c=-g_2$ and $R\ge4$: by Lemma~\ref{lem:chain},
 $\psi_{b-1}=2P(1+\mu)(R-1)^2$, and
 $\psi_{b-1}-4R(P-\mu)\ge2P(R^2-4R+1)>0$.
\end{itemize}

Now let $c=-g_2$ and $R=2$, so that $p=3$ and $P\ge4$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:chain}, $\psi_{b-1}=2P(1+\mu_{b-2})$ and
$\zeta_{b-2}=-\mu_0g_2$, so $B'=M\zeta_{b-2}=m\,(12,4,-7)$ with $m=\mu_0<1$.
We show that $\psi_{b-2}\ge2P(1+\mu')$, where $\mu'=\mu_{b-3}$, for every
column $c'=c_{b-2}$. Here $Mg_1=(12,-8,5)$, $Mg_2=(-12,-4,7)$,
$Mg_3=(0,-12,3)$ and $Mg_4=(0,0,9)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:22}
22(P-\mu')m\ge2P(1+\mu'),
\end{equation}
because $x\cdot22(P-\mu')m=22(P-\mu')(P-1)\ge22(P-1)^2\ge4P(P+1)\ge
2P(1+\mu')x$; indeed $22(P-1)^2-4P(P+1)=18P^2-48P+22>0$ for $P\ge4$. Let
$h'_i$ be the cells of $\psi_{b-2}$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $c'=\pm g_4$: by~\eqref{eq:S34},
 $\psi_{b-2}\ge16P(1+\mu')-32\mu'm\ge2P(1+\mu')+14P(1+\mu')-32\mu'
 >2P(1+\mu')$.
\item $c'=\pm g_3$: by~\eqref{eq:S34},
 $\psi_{b-2}\ge10P(1+\mu')-24\mu'm>2P(1+\mu')$.
\item $c'=\pm g_2$: $|B'_i|\le|(Mc')_i|$ for all $i$, so all cells are
 $\le0$ and $\psi_{b-2}\ge P(1+\mu')\Delta_2=2P(1+\mu')$.
\item $c'=s_2$: $h'_0=-24(P-\mu')m$ and $h'_1,h'_2\le0$ by
 Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol}, so $\psi_{b-2}\ge24(P-\mu')m$, and~\eqref{eq:22}
 applies.
\item $c'=-s_2$: then $Mc'=(-12,8,-5)$, $h'_0\le0$ and $h'_1=-8(P-\mu')m$.
 If $7m\le5$, then $h'_2=-14(P-\mu')m$ and $\psi_{b-2}\ge22(P-\mu')m$. If
 $7m>5$, then $h'_2=14\mu'm-10P$ by~\eqref{eq:hmore}, and
 $\psi_{b-2}\ge8(P-\mu')m-14\mu'm+10P\ge8Pm-22m+10P>4P\ge2P(1+\mu')$.
\end{itemize}
Finally put $u=\mu_{b-2}$ and $v=\mu_{b-3}$, so that $xu=P-v$ and
$x\rho=P-u$. By~\eqref{eq:muinf}, $v>\mu_\infty=P/(P+2)$, since $b-3$ is odd
or $v=\mu_{-1}=1$. Using $xu=P-v$, one finds
\[
x\bigl[2P(1+u)+2P(1+v)-8(P-u)\bigr]=2(P-2)\bigl[(P+2)v-P\bigr]>0.
\]
Hence $\psi_{b-1}+\psi_{b-2}\ge2P(1+u)+2P(1+v)>8x\rho=4Rx\rho$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:signB}]
Let $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{3\times(b+1)}$ with $y_{2b}=-1$. By
Lemmas~\ref{lem:onecol} and~\ref{lem:colS}, all $\psi_j$ are nonnegative, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:colid} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budgetB}
x^{1-b}\bigl(\Theta-G(Y)\bigr)\ge\sum_{j<b}\psi_j
+x\rho\bigl(\Delta(c_b)-2\delta_2(p)\bigr).
\end{equation}
If $Y=Y^-$, then $G(Y)=\Theta$. Otherwise let $J$ be the largest index with
$c_J\ne(-1)^{J+1}s_2$.

If $J<b$, then $c_b=-s_2$ as $b$ is even, so $\Delta(c_b)=0$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:chain}, $\zeta_J=\varepsilon\mu_{b-2-J}s_2$ with
$\varepsilon=(-1)^J$, so
$\psi_J=\psi_{\mu_{J-1}}(\varepsilon c_J,\mu_{b-2-J}s_2)$ with
$\varepsilon c_J\ne-s_2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fd1},
$\psi_J>2x\delta_2(p)\rho$, and~\eqref{eq:budgetB} gives $G(Y)<\Theta$.

If $J=b$, then $c_b\ne-s_2$ and $y_{2b}=-1$, so $c_b$ is $g_2$, $g_3$ or
$-g_4$. If $c_b=-g_4$, then $\Delta(c_b)-2\delta_2(p)=2R^2-2>0$
by~\eqref{eq:Deltas}, and $G(Y)<\Theta$. If $c_b=g_2$, then
$\Delta(c_b)-2\delta_2(p)=-4R$, and $G(Y)<\Theta$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:fd2}. If
$c_b=g_3=g_+$, then Lemma~\ref{lem:colid} gives
$x^{1-b}(\Theta-G(Y))=\sum_j\psi_j+2x((M\zeta_{-1})_2)^-\ge0$. If equality
holds, all $\psi_j$ vanish, so every $c_j$ with $j<b$ is $\pm s_2$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:colS}, and $Y=Y^+$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:eqgen}. Conversely
$G(Y^+)=\Theta$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:eqgen}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:tightB}
Two families have explicit gaps. For $b=2$, $q=3$ and
$Y=s_2(1,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$, which is case~(a) of Lemma~\ref{lem:fd1} with
$J=0$, we have $\Theta-G(Y)=4(p-3)$ (Remark~\ref{rem:near}). For $p=3$ and
$Y=(1,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}s_b^{\mathsf T}$, whose columns are $\pm g_2$, we have
$\Theta-G(Y)=2\bigl(d_b(q)-5\delta_b(q)\bigr)$, which is $4(q-3)$ for $b=2$.
\end{remark}

\section{A conditional reduction for larger exponents}\label{sec:minfour}
This section proves no case of Conjecture~\ref{conj:equal} by itself. We use the
notation of Section~\ref{sec:column}.
For a fixed exponent $a\ge2$ of~$p$, and for all real $p,q$
satisfying~\eqref{eq:regions} in one of its two regions,
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} deduces the following conclusion from the
conditions (S$_a$), (F$_a$) and (C$_a$), which do not depend on~$b$: for all
$b\ge1$ with $b\equiv a\pmod2$, every $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}$ with
$y_{ab}=-1$ satisfies $G(Y)\le\Theta$, with equality exactly for $Y^-$ and~$Y^+$.
The proposition is conditional. Section~\ref{sec:three} proves, for every
$a\ge3$, condition (S$_a$) on both regions, condition (C$_a$) at every point of
$\mathcal R_2$, and condition (F$_a$) at the points $(3,q)$ with $q=5$, $q=7$ or
$q\ge8$; this suffices for Theorem~\ref{thm:minfour} when one of the primes is~$3$.
We do not claim (F$_a$) or (C$_a$) at any other point with $a\ge3$. Version~1.5
verified the conditions for $3\le a\le8$ by computer, and that verification is not
part of this version.

Let $\mathcal R_1$ and $\mathcal R_2$ be the two sets of pairs $(p,q)$
in~\eqref{eq:regions}, and put $P_{\min}=2$ on $\mathcal R_1$ and
$P_{\min}=4$ on $\mathcal R_2$. For real $y$ let $\eta_0(y)=y$ and
$\eta_{n+1}(y)=(P-\eta_n(y))/x$, so that $\mu_{j+n}=\eta_n(\mu_j)$. Put
$\hat D_1=d_1(x)/x=(3x-4)/x$ and $\hat D_2=d_2(x)/x^2=(5x^2-8x+4)/x^2$. For
$a$ even, let $\mathcal F_a$ be the set of the triples $(\mu,m,r)$ of the
forms $(1,m,(P-m)/x)$ with $\mu_0\le m\le\mu_\infty$; $(\mu,1,(P-\mu)/x)$ with
$\mu_0\le\mu\le\mu_\infty$; $(\mu,m,\mu)$ with $\mu_\infty\le\mu\le\mu_1$ and
$\mu_0\le m\le\mu_\infty$; and $(\mu,m,m)$ with $\mu_0\le\mu\le\mu_\infty$ and
$\mu_\infty\le m\le\mu_1$. For $a$ odd, let $\mathcal F_a$ consist of
$(1,1,\mu_0)$; the triples $(1,m,(P-m)/x)$ and $(\mu,1,(P-\mu)/x)$ with
$\mu_\infty\le m,\mu\le\mu_1$; and the triples $(\mu,m,\mu_\infty)$ with
$\mu,m$ both in $[\mu_0,\mu_\infty]$ or both in $[\mu_\infty,\mu_1]$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reduction}
Let $a\ge2$, and suppose that the following conditions hold at every point
of $\mathcal R_1$, or at every point of $\mathcal R_2$.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(S$_a$)] $P_{\min}\Delta(c)>\sum_{i=0}^a
 \max\{0,\ \omega_i-P_{\min}|(Mc)_i|\}$ for every $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$
 other than $\pm s_a$.
\item[(F$_a$)] $\psi_\mu(c,ms_a)>2x\delta_a(p)\,r$ for every
 $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ other than $-s_a$ and every $(\mu,m,r)\in\mathcal F_a$.
\item[(C$_a$)] Every $g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ with $g_a=-1$, other than
 $-(-1)^as_a$ and $g_+$, satisfies $\Delta(g)>2\delta_a(p)$ or the following
 three conditions, where $\gamma=2\delta_a(p)-\Delta(g)$.
 \begin{itemize}
 \item[(C1)] For $0\le k\le4$, every $y\in I_k$ and every
 $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ other than $(-1)^{k+1}g$,
 \[
 \sum_{n=1}^{k}P\bigl(1+\eta_n(y)\bigr)\Delta(g)
 +\psi_y\bigl(c,(-1)^k\mu_{k-1}g\bigr)>x\,\eta_{k+1}(y)\,\gamma,
 \]
 where $I_k=[\mu_0,\mu_\infty]$ if $k\equiv a\pmod2$, and
 $I_k=[\mu_\infty,\mu_1]\cup\{1\}$ otherwise.
 \item[(C2)] $\sum_{n=0}^{4}P\bigl(1+\eta_n(y)\bigr)\Delta(g)
 >x\,\eta_5(y)\,\gamma$ for every $y\in[\mu_0,\mu_\infty]$ if $a$ is even,
 and for every $y\in[\mu_\infty,\mu_1]$ if $a$ is odd.
 \item[(C3)] $\Delta(g)\bigl(\hat D_1/\mu_1+2\bigr)>2\delta_a(p)$ if $a$ is
 even; $\Delta(g)\hat D_1/\mu_0>2\delta_a(p)$ and
 $\Delta(g)\bigl(\hat D_2/\mu_\infty+2\bigr)>2\delta_a(p)$ if $a$ is odd.
 \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
Then at every point of that region and for every $b\ge1$ with
$b\equiv a\pmod2$, every $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}$ with $y_{ab}=-1$
satisfies $G(Y)\le\Theta$, with equality exactly for $Y^-$ and $Y^+$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First, $\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)>0$ for all $c\ne\pm s_a$, $0\le\mu\le1$ and
$\zeta\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$. Indeed, by Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol} and
$|(M\zeta)_i|\le\omega_i$, $\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)$ is at least
$P(1+\mu)\Delta(c)-2\mu\sum_i\max\{0,\omega_i-P|(Mc)_i|\}$, which is affine in
$\mu$; at $\mu=0$ it equals $P\Delta(c)>0$, and at $\mu=1$ it is at least twice
the difference of the two sides of~(S$_a$), since $P\ge P_{\min}$. So, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol}, all $\psi_j$ are nonnegative, $\psi_j=0$ only if
$c_j=\pm s_a$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:colid} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budgetA}
x^{1-b}\bigl(\Theta-G(Y)\bigr)\ge\sum_{j<b}\psi_j
+x\rho\bigl(\Delta(c_b)-2\delta_a(p)\bigr).
\end{equation}
If $Y=Y^-$, then $G(Y)=\Theta$. Otherwise let $J$ be the largest index with
$c_J\ne(-1)^{J+1}s_a$.

\emph{Case $J<b$.} Then $\Delta(c_b)=0$, and as in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:signB}, $\psi_J=\psi_\mu(\varepsilon c_J,ms_a)$ with
$\mu=\mu_{J-1}$, $m=\mu_{b-2-J}$, $\varepsilon=(-1)^J$ and
$\varepsilon c_J\ne-s_a$. We claim that $(\mu,m,r)\in\mathcal F_a$ for some
$r\ge\rho$; then $\psi_J>2x\delta_a(p)\rho$ by~(F$_a$), and $G(Y)<\Theta$
by~\eqref{eq:budgetA}. Let $a$, and hence $b$, be even. If $J=0$, then
$\mu=\mu_{-1}=1$, $m=\mu_{b-2}\in[\mu_0,\mu_\infty]$ by~\eqref{eq:muinf},
and $\rho=(P-m)/x$. If $J=b-1$, then $m=1$, $\mu=\mu_{b-2}$ and
$\rho=(P-\mu)/x$. If $1\le J\le b-2$, the indices $J-1$ and $b-2-J$ are
nonnegative with odd sum; by~\eqref{eq:muinf}, the value with the even index
lies in $[\mu_0,\mu_\infty]$ and the value with the odd index lies in
$[\mu_\infty,\mu_1]$ and exceeds $\rho=\mu_{b-1}$, since the $\mu_j$ with odd
$j$ decrease. Now let $a$ be odd. If $b=1$, then $J=0$ and
$(\mu,m,\rho)=(1,1,\mu_0)$. If $b\ge3$, the cases $J=0$ and $J=b-1$ are as
before, now with $\mu_{b-2}\in[\mu_\infty,\mu_1]$; and if $1\le J\le b-2$,
the indices $J-1$ and $b-2-J$ have the same parity, while
$\rho=\mu_{b-1}<\mu_\infty$ as $b-1$ is even.

\emph{Case $J=b$.} Then $g=c_b$ satisfies $g_a=-1$ and $g\ne-(-1)^as_a$. If
$g=g_+$, then Lemmas~\ref{lem:colid} and~\ref{lem:eqgen} give
$x^{1-b}(\Theta-G(Y))=\sum_j\psi_j+2x((M\zeta_{-1})_a)^-\ge0$; equality forces
all $\psi_j$ to vanish, so that $c_j=\pm s_a$ for $j<b$ and $Y=Y^+$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:eqgen}, while $G(Y^+)=\Theta$. If $\Delta(g)>2\delta_a(p)$,
then $G(Y)<\Theta$ by~\eqref{eq:budgetA}. Otherwise (C1)--(C3) hold, and
by~\eqref{eq:budgetA} it suffices to show $\sum_j\psi_j>x\rho\gamma$. Let $k$
be the largest integer with $k\le b$ and $c_{b-i}=(-1)^ig$ for $1\le i\le k$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:chain}, $\psi_{b-i}=P(1+\mu_{b-i-1})\Delta(g)$ for
$1\le i\le k$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $k<b$ and $k\le4$, put $y=\mu_{b-2-k}$, so that
 $\mu_{b-i-1}=\eta_{k+1-i}(y)$ and $\rho=\eta_{k+1}(y)$. Then $y\in I_k$:
 if $b-2-k\ge0$, by~\eqref{eq:muinf}, as $b-2-k\equiv a-k\pmod2$; and if
 $b=k+1$, then $y=\mu_{-1}=1$ and $k\not\equiv a\pmod2$. The column
 $j=b-1-k$ satisfies $c_j\ne(-1)^{k+1}g$ and, by Lemma~\ref{lem:chain},
 $\zeta_j=(-1)^k\mu_{k-1}g$; so $\psi_j=\psi_y(c_j,(-1)^k\mu_{k-1}g)$, and
 (C1) gives $\sum_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{b-i}+\psi_j>x\rho\gamma$.
\item If $5\le k<b$, then $b\ge6$, and $y=\mu_{b-6}$ lies in
 $[\mu_0,\mu_\infty]$ if $a$ is even and in $[\mu_\infty,\mu_1]$ if $a$ is
 odd. The columns $b-1,\dots,b-5$ give
 $\sum_{i=1}^{5}\psi_{b-i}=\sum_{n=0}^{4}P(1+\eta_n(y))\Delta(g)$, and
 $\rho=\eta_5(y)$, so (C2) applies.
\item If $k=b$, then $\sum_j\psi_j=x\Delta(g)(\hat D-\rho)$, and the claim
 reads $\Delta(g)\hat D/\rho>2\delta_a(p)$. By Remark~\ref{rem:closed},
 $\hat D=\hat D_1+2\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}\mu_i$. If $b$ is even, then
 $\rho=\mu_{b-1}\le\mu_1$, so $\hat D/\rho\ge\hat D_1/\mu_1+2$; if $b=1$,
 then $\hat D/\rho=\hat D_1/\mu_0$; and if $b\ge3$ is odd, then
 $\hat D\ge\hat D_2+2\mu_{b-1}$ and $\rho<\mu_\infty$, so
 $\hat D/\rho\ge\hat D_2/\mu_\infty+2$. Since $\Delta(g)>0$, (C3) applies.
 \qedhere
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\section{Exponents of equal parity when one prime is~3}\label{sec:three}
In this section we prove the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} at the
points $(3,q)$ that are needed for Theorem~\ref{thm:minfour} when one of the primes
is~$3$, for every exponent $a\ge3$ of the prime~$3$, and we deduce that case of the
theorem. The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} uses the three conditions
only at the point $(p,q)$ under consideration, and it uses $P_{\min}$ only through
the inequality $P\ge P_{\min}$. So its conclusion holds at every point of
$\mathcal R_1$ (respectively $\mathcal R_2$) at which (S$_a$) with $P_{\min}=2$
(respectively $P_{\min}=4$), (F$_a$) and (C$_a$) hold; we use it in this pointwise
form. The names of Lean lemmas given below refer to the modules
\texttt{Research/TaskE/*.lean} of Section~\ref{sec:verif} (namespaces
\texttt{CodexPaper1.TaskE}, \texttt{CodexPaper1.TaskE.SProof} and \texttt{CodexPaper1.TaskE.FCProof}), except
where another module is named.

We keep the notation of Sections~\ref{sec:column} and~\ref{sec:minfour}: $x=q$,
$P=x-1$, $\mu_j$ is the potential for~$x$, $\mu_\infty=P/(x+1)$, and $\eta_n$,
$\mathcal F_a$, $I_k$, $M=T_a(p)$, $\omega_i$, $\Delta$, $\psi_\mu$ and its cells
are as there. In addition, for real $p\ge3$ and $a\ge1$ let $L=p^{a-1}(p-1)$; for
$w\in\mathbb R^{a+1}$ let $\lambda_j(w)$, $-1\le j\le a-1$, be the
path~\eqref{eq:path} with $x=p$ and $k=a$; let $\bar\mu_j=\mu^{(p)}_j$ be the
potential of Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} for~$p$; and for $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ put
$\operatorname{pen}_i(c)=\max\{0,\ \omega_i-2|(Mc)_i|\}$, $0\le i\le a$. By the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:path}, by Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} and as
in~\eqref{eq:Mfacts}, for $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $0\le j<a$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lamfacts}
(Mc)_{a-1-j}=L\bigl(\lambda_j(c)-c_j\bigr),\quad (Mc)_a=p^a\lambda_{-1}(c),\quad
\operatorname{sgn}\lambda_j(c)=c_{j+1},\quad \tfrac{p-2}{p}\le|\lambda_j(c)|\le1,
\end{equation}
where the last two statements hold also for $j=-1$; moreover $\omega_i=2L$ for
$i<a$, $\omega_a=p^a$, $\bar\mu_j\ge\frac{p-2}p$ for $j\ge-1$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Deltaid}
\Delta(c)=2p^a\sum_{j\in\NA(c)}\bar\mu_j\,|\lambda_j(c)|.
\end{equation}
In Lean these are \texttt{row\_abs\_mass}, \texttt{row\_path},
\texttt{path\_bounds}, \texttt{terminal\_lower}, \texttt{mu\_lower} and
\texttt{deficit\_identity}.

\paragraph{Condition (S$_a$).} This condition does not involve~$q$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pen}
Let $a\ge1$, let $p\ge3$ be real and $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$. For $0\le j<a$,
$\operatorname{pen}_{a-1-j}(c)=2L|\lambda_j(c)|$ if $j\in\NA(c)$, and
$\operatorname{pen}_{a-1-j}(c)=0$ otherwise. Consequently
\[
2\Delta(c)-\sum_{i<a}\operatorname{pen}_i(c)
=2p^{a-1}\sum_{j\in\NA(c)}\bigl(2p\bar\mu_j-(p-1)\bigr)|\lambda_j(c)|,
\]
which is nonnegative, and positive if $p\ge5$ and $c\ne\pm s_a$. Moreover
$\max\{0,\ \omega_a-K|(Mc)_a|\}=0$ for every $K\ge0$ with $p\le K(p-2)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $i=a-1-j$. By~\eqref{eq:lamfacts}, $|(Mc)_i|=L|\lambda_j(c)-c_j|$, where
$\lambda_j(c)$ has the sign $c_{j+1}$ and absolute value at most~$1$. If
$c_j\ne c_{j+1}$, then $|\lambda_j(c)-c_j|=1+|\lambda_j(c)|$, so
$2|(Mc)_i|\ge2L=\omega_i$ and $\operatorname{pen}_i(c)=0$. If $c_j=c_{j+1}$, then
$|\lambda_j(c)-c_j|=1-|\lambda_j(c)|$ and $\operatorname{pen}_i(c)=2L|\lambda_j(c)|$.
Summing over~$j$ and subtracting from twice~\eqref{eq:Deltaid} gives the identity:
each $j\in\NA(c)$ contributes
$4p^a\bar\mu_j|\lambda_j(c)|-2L|\lambda_j(c)|=2p^{a-1}\bigl(2p\bar\mu_j-(p-1)\bigr)|\lambda_j(c)|$. As $\bar\mu_j\ge\frac{p-2}p$,
$2p\bar\mu_j-(p-1)\ge p-3$, which is $\ge0$, and $>0$ if $p\ge5$; if $c\ne\pm
s_a$, then $\NA(c)\ne\emptyset$ and $|\lambda_j(c)|>0$. Finally
$|(Mc)_a|=p^a|\lambda_{-1}(c)|\ge p^{a-1}(p-2)$, so $K|(Mc)_a|\ge
p^{a-1}K(p-2)\ge p^a=\omega_a$. (Lean: \texttt{penalty\_row},
\texttt{penalty\_identity}, \texttt{budget\_gap}, \texttt{penalty\_bound},
\texttt{penalty\_strict}, \texttt{terminal\_penalty\_zero}.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:S}
Let $a\ge1$. Condition~(S$_a$) holds with $P_{\min}=4$ for every real $p\ge3$, and
with $P_{\min}=2$ for every real $p\ge5$. In particular it holds at every point of
$\mathcal R_1$ with $P_{\min}=2$ and at every point of $\mathcal R_2$ with
$P_{\min}=4$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $c\ne\pm s_a$, so that $\Delta(c)>0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:potential}. If
$P_{\min}=4$ and $p\ge3$, then $p\le4(p-2)$, so the term $i=a$ of the sum in
(S$_a$) vanishes by Lemma~\ref{lem:pen}; the terms with $i<a$ are at most
$\operatorname{pen}_i(c)$, and their sum is at most $2\Delta(c)<4\Delta(c)$. If
$P_{\min}=2$ and $p\ge5$, then $p\le2(p-2)$, the term $i=a$ vanishes again, and
the sum of the other terms, $\sum_{i<a}\operatorname{pen}_i(c)$, is smaller than
$2\Delta(c)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:pen}. (Lean: \texttt{condS\_four},
\texttt{condS\_two}, \texttt{check\_ConditionsSUniform}.)
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The prime $3$.} From now on $p=3$, $a\ge3$, and $q\ge5$ is real, so that
$(3,q)\in\mathcal R_2$, $P\ge4$, $L=2\cdot3^{a-1}$ and $3^a=\frac32L$. By
Section~\ref{sec:potential}, Remark~\ref{rem:closed} and~\eqref{eq:muinf} with
$x=3$, where $\mu_\infty=\frac12$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threefacts}
\bar\mu_0=\tfrac13,\ \ \bar\mu_1=\tfrac59,\ \ \tfrac13\le\bar\mu_j\le\tfrac59\
(j\ge0),\ \ \bar\mu_j\ge\tfrac49\ (j\ge1),\ \
2\delta_a(3)=2\cdot3^a\bar\mu_{a-1}=3^a+(-1)^a,
\end{equation}
$\bar\mu_{-1}=1$, $\bar\mu_{j-1}=2-3\bar\mu_j$, and $\bar\mu_j>\frac12$ for odd
$j$. For $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $0\le j<a$, the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} gives $|\lambda_{j-1}(c)|=(2+|\lambda_j(c)|)/3$ if
$j\in\NA(c)$ and $|\lambda_{j-1}(c)|=(2-|\lambda_j(c)|)/3$ otherwise. If
$c_l\ne c_{l+1}$ for $J<l<a$, then $|\lambda_J(c)|=\bar\mu_{a-2-J}$, as in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:paritydefect}. If $c'\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $c'_l=-c_l$ for
$J<l\le a$, then $\lambda_l(c')=-\lambda_l(c)$ for $J\le l\le a-1$, since
$\lambda_l(c)$ is a linear function of $c_{l+1},\dots,c_a$. We also use the values
of the cell function at arguments of the same sign: for $P\ge0$ and $A,B\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hsame}
h_\mu(A,B)=2\mu B-2PA\ \ \text{if } A\le B,\qquad
h_\mu(A,B)=-2(P-\mu)B\ \ \text{if } B\le A,
\end{equation}
directly from the definition, and, as $h_\mu(-A,-B)=h_\mu(A,B)$, the same with
$|A|,|B|$ in place of $A,B$ if $A,B\le0$ (Lean: \texttt{hcell\_same\_small},
\texttt{hcell\_same\_large}, \texttt{hcell\_sign}). We put
$K(\mu)=P(1+\mu)-4\mu=P+\mu(P-4)$, so that $K(\mu)\ge P$ for $\mu\ge0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gain}
Let $0\le\mu\le1$, $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $\zeta\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$, and let
$h_i=h_\mu\bigl((Mc)_i,(M\zeta)_i\bigr)$ be the cells of $\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)$. Then
$h_i\le2\mu\operatorname{pen}_i(c)$ for $i<a$, and $h_a\le0$. Consequently, for
every $i<a$,
\[
\psi_\mu(c,\zeta)\ \ge\ K(\mu)\,\Delta(c)+\bigl(2\mu\operatorname{pen}_i(c)-h_i\bigr)
\ \ge\ K(\mu)\,\Delta(c)\ \ge\ P\,\Delta(c).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:cell}, which applies as $P\ge4>\mu$, and by
$|(M\zeta)_i|\le\omega_i$, we have $h_i\le2\mu\max\{0,\ \omega_i-P|(Mc)_i|\}$. For
$i<a$ this is at most $2\mu\operatorname{pen}_i(c)$ because $P\ge2$. For $i=a$ it
vanishes by the last part of Lemma~\ref{lem:pen} with $K=P$, as $3\le P$. Hence
$\sum_lh_l\le h_i+2\mu\sum_{l<a,\,l\ne i}\operatorname{pen}_l(c)
\le h_i-2\mu\operatorname{pen}_i(c)+4\mu\Delta(c)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:pen}, and the
definition of $\psi_\mu$ gives the first inequality. The second holds because
$h_i\le2\mu\operatorname{pen}_i(c)$, and the third because $\Delta(c)\ge0$.
(Lean: \texttt{hcell\_row\_budget}, \texttt{hcell\_terminal\_nonpos},
\texttt{psi\_row\_gain}, \texttt{potential\_lower}.)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:deficit3}
Let $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] $\Delta(c)\ge2\cdot3^a\bar\mu_j|\lambda_j(c)|$ for every $j\in\NA(c)$.
 If $c_{a-1}=c_a$, then $\Delta(c)\ge2\delta_a(3)$.
\item[(ii)] If $c\ne\pm s_a$, then $\Delta(c)\ge\frac8{27}3^a$; if moreover $a$ is
 odd, then $\Delta(c)\ge\frac13 3^a$.
\item[(iii)] $2\delta_a(3)\le\frac{28}{27}3^a$ and $2\delta_a(3)\le\frac{41}{27}L$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(i) All terms of~\eqref{eq:Deltaid} are nonnegative. If $c_{a-1}=c_a$, the term
$j=a-1$ is $2\cdot3^a\bar\mu_{a-1}=2\delta_a(3)$, as $|\lambda_{a-1}(c)|=|c_a|=1$.

(ii) If some $j\ge1$ lies in $\NA(c)$, then $\bar\mu_j\ge\frac49$ and
$|\lambda_j(c)|\ge\frac13$, and (i) gives $\Delta(c)\ge\frac8{27}3^a$. Otherwise
$\NA(c)=\{0\}$, as $c\ne\pm s_a$; since $1,2\notin\NA(c)$ and $a\ge3$,
$|\lambda_1(c)|=(2-|\lambda_2(c)|)/3\le\frac59$ and
$|\lambda_0(c)|=(2-|\lambda_1(c)|)/3\ge\frac{13}{27}\ge\frac49$, so (i) with $j=0$
gives $\Delta(c)\ge2\cdot3^a\cdot\frac13\cdot\frac49$. Let $a$ be odd and
$J=\max\NA(c)$. Then $c$ alternates after~$J$, so $|\lambda_J(c)|=\bar\mu_{a-2-J}$.
The indices $J\ge0$ and $a-2-J\ge-1$ have the odd sum $a-2$, so one of them is odd
or equal to $-1$, and the corresponding value of $\bar\mu$ exceeds~$\frac12$,
while the other is at least~$\frac13$. By (i),
$\Delta(c)\ge2\cdot3^a\cdot\frac16$.

(iii) By~\eqref{eq:threefacts}, $2\delta_a(3)\le3^a+1\le\frac{28}{27}3^a$ as
$3^a\ge27$. If $a$ is odd, $2\delta_a(3)<3^a=\frac32L<\frac{41}{27}L$. If $a$ is
even, then $a\ge4$, $L\ge54$ and $2\delta_a(3)=\frac32L+1\le(\frac32+\frac1{54})L
=\frac{41}{27}L$. (Lean: \texttt{deficit\_pays\_term},
\texttt{terminal\_repeat\_high}, \texttt{deficit\_lower},
\texttt{deficit\_odd\_lower}, \texttt{two\_delta\_upper}, \texttt{delta\_ratio}.)
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Three row computations.} The next lemmas evaluate one cell exactly and
insert it into Lemma~\ref{lem:gain}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lastpair}
Let $0\le\mu\le1$ and $0\le m\le1$, and let $c,w\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ satisfy
$c_a=w_a$, $c_{a-1}=-c_a$ and $w_{a-1}=-w_a$. Then
$\psi_\mu(c,mw)\ge4(P-\mu)mL$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:lamfacts} with $j=a-1$ and $\lambda_{a-1}(c)=c_a$,
$(Mc)_0=L(c_a-c_{a-1})=2Lc_a$, and likewise $(Mw)_0=2Lc_a$. So
$\operatorname{pen}_0(c)=0$, the arguments of the cell $h_0$ are $2Lc_a$ and
$2mLc_a$, and $h_0=-4(P-\mu)mL$ by~\eqref{eq:hsame}. Lemma~\ref{lem:gain} with
$i=0$ gives the claim, as $K(\mu)\Delta(c)\ge0$. (Lean:
\texttt{last\_pair\_F\_lower}, \texttt{alt\_last\_pair\_gain},
\texttt{common\_last\_pair\_gain}; they prove the three instances used below.)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:alttail}
Let $0\le J\le a-2$ and let $w\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ satisfy $w_J=(s_a)_J$ and
$w_l=-(s_a)_l$ for $J<l\le a$. Put $\theta=|\lambda_J(s_a)|$, $i=a-1-J$ and
$\varepsilon=-(s_a)_J$. Then $\theta=\bar\mu_{a-2-J}\in[\frac13,\frac59]$,
$|\lambda_J(w)|=\theta$, $J\in\NA(w)$, no $l>J$ lies in $\NA(w)$,
$\Delta(w)\ge2\cdot3^a\bar\mu_J\theta\ge L\theta$,
\[
(Mw)_i=\varepsilon L(1-\theta),\qquad (Ms_a)_i=\varepsilon L(1+\theta).
\]
Moreover, for $0\le y\le1$ and $0\le m\le1$, all cells of $\psi_y(s_a,mw)$ are
$\le0$, the cell in row~$i$ equals $-2(P-y)mL(1-\theta)$, and
$\psi_y(s_a,mw)\ge2(P-y)mL(1-\theta)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
As $s_a$ alternates, $\theta=\bar\mu_{a-2-J}$, which lies in $[\frac13,\frac59]$
by~\eqref{eq:threefacts}. Since $w=-s_a$ after~$J$,
$\lambda_J(w)=-\lambda_J(s_a)$, and $\lambda_J(s_a)$ has the sign
$(s_a)_{J+1}=\varepsilon$; so $\lambda_J(s_a)=\varepsilon\theta$ and
$\lambda_J(w)=-\varepsilon\theta$. Also $w_J=(s_a)_J=-(s_a)_{J+1}=w_{J+1}$, and
$w_l\ne w_{l+1}$ for $l>J$. Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit3}(i) and $\bar\mu_J\ge\frac13$
give $\Delta(w)\ge2\cdot3^{a-1}\theta=L\theta$. By~\eqref{eq:lamfacts},
$(Mw)_i=L(-\varepsilon\theta+\varepsilon)$ and
$(Ms_a)_i=L(\varepsilon\theta+\varepsilon)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:onecol}, all cells

\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:tailbudget}
Let $g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$, $0\le J<a$ and $\theta=|\lambda_J(g)|$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tailbudget}
2\delta_a(3)\le2\cdot3^a\Bigl(\bar\mu_J\theta+2\sum_{l\in\NA(g),\,l>J}
\bar\mu_l|\lambda_l(g)|\Bigr)+2L(1-\theta),
\end{equation}
and the inequality is strict if $J\ge1$ and $\theta<1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $\theta_j=|\lambda_j(g)|$ and $\beta_j=1-\bar\mu_j+(2\bar\mu_j-1)\theta_j$
for $-1\le j\le a-1$. Let $0\le l<a$. If $l\notin\NA(g)$, then
$\theta_{l-1}=(2-\theta_l)/3$, and with $\bar\mu_{l-1}=2-3\bar\mu_l$ one finds
$\beta_{l-1}=\beta_l$. If $l\in\NA(g)$, then $\theta_{l-1}=(2+\theta_l)/3$, and
one finds $\beta_l-\beta_{l-1}=2(2\bar\mu_l-1)\theta_l\le2\bar\mu_l\theta_l$, as
$\bar\mu_l\le1$. Adding these relations for $J<l\le a-1$, and using
$\theta_{a-1}=1$, so that $\beta_{a-1}=\bar\mu_{a-1}$, we get
\[
\bar\mu_{a-1}\le\beta_J+2\sum_{l\in\NA(g),\,l>J}\bar\mu_l\theta_l.
\]
Finally $\beta_J=\bar\mu_J\theta+(1-\bar\mu_J)(1-\theta)\le\bar\mu_J\theta+
\frac23(1-\theta)$, because $\bar\mu_J\ge\frac13$, and the inequality is strict
if $\bar\mu_J>\frac13$ and $\theta<1$, in particular if $J\ge1$ and $\theta<1$,
by~\eqref{eq:threefacts}. Multiplying by $2\cdot3^a$, and using
$2\cdot3^a\bar\mu_{a-1}=2\delta_a(3)$ and $2\cdot3^a\cdot\frac23=2L$, gives the
lemma. (Lean: \texttt{repeat\_abs\_step}, \texttt{potential\_step\_budget},
\texttt{potential\_tail\_budget}, \texttt{zero\_tail\_budget},
\texttt{zero\_tail\_budget\_strict}.)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pair}
Let $0\le y\le1$, let $c,g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ and $0\le J<a$ with $c_J=g_J$ and
$c_l=-g_l$ for $J<l\le a$, and put $\theta=|\lambda_J(g)|$. Then
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] $2\delta_a(3)\le\Delta(c)+\Delta(g)+2L(1-\theta)$;
\item[(ii)] $\psi_y(c,g)\ge K(y)\,\Delta(c)+2(P-y)L(1-\theta)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(i) We have $\lambda_l(c)=-\lambda_l(g)$ for $J\le l\le a-1$; in particular
$|\lambda_J(c)|=\theta$. For $l>J$, $l\in\NA(c)$ if and only if $l\in\NA(g)$, since
$c_l=-g_l$ and $c_{l+1}=-g_{l+1}$. As $c_J=g_J$ and $c_{J+1}=-g_{J+1}$, the
index~$J$ lies in exactly one of $\NA(c)$ and $\NA(g)$. Hence, by~\eqref{eq:Deltaid},
$\Delta(c)+\Delta(g)$ is at least the first term on the right
of~\eqref{eq:tailbudget}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:tailbudget} gives~(i).

(ii) Put $i=a-1-J$ and $\chi=c_J\lambda_J(g)\in[-1,1]$, so that $|\chi|=\theta$.
By~\eqref{eq:lamfacts}, $(Mc)_i=L(-\lambda_J(g)-c_J)=-c_JL(1+\chi)$ and
$(Mg)_i=L(\lambda_J(g)-c_J)=-c_JL(1-\chi)$, and $\omega_i=2L$. If $\chi\ge0$, then
$\operatorname{pen}_i(c)=0$ and, by~\eqref{eq:hsame},
$h_i=-2(P-y)L(1-\chi)$. If $\chi<0$, then $\operatorname{pen}_i(c)=2L|\chi|$ and
$h_i=2yL(1+|\chi|)-2PL(1-|\chi|)$. In both cases
$2y\operatorname{pen}_i(c)-h_i=2(P-y)L(1-\theta)$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:gain} gives
(ii). (Lean: \texttt{opposite\_tail\_z}, \texttt{opposite\_tail\_NA},
\texttt{deficit\_tail\_at}, \texttt{pair\_match\_budget},
\texttt{matched\_row\_gain}, \texttt{pair\_match\_gain}.)
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Condition (F$_a$).} We prove it for the real numbers
\[
q=5,\qquad q=7\qquad\text{and}\qquad q\ge8;
\]
these include every odd prime $q\ne3$. We do not claim it for other real $q\ge5$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Fscalar}
Let $q=5$, $q=7$ or $q\ge8$, and $(\mu,m,r)\in\mathcal F_a$. Then
$\mu_0\le\mu\le1$, $\mu_0\le m\le1$, $0<xr<P$ and $\mu_0\ge\frac35$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fscalar}
\tfrac{41}{27}\,xr<2P-\tfrac59P(1-\mu)-\tfrac{28}9\mu m.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The bounds follow from the definition of $\mathcal F_a$ and~\eqref{eq:murange}:
$\mu_0=(P-1)/x\ge\frac35$, $\mu_\infty\le\mu_1\le1$, and $r$ is one of $(P-m)/x$,
$(P-\mu)/x$, $\mu$, $m$, $\mu_0$, $\mu_\infty$, each of which is positive and,
multiplied by~$x$, smaller than $P$, because $x\mu_1=P-\mu_0$. If $q\ge8$, then
$\mu\ge\mu_0\ge\frac34$, and by $xr<P$ and $m\le1$ the right side
of~\eqref{eq:Fscalar} minus $\frac{41}{27}xr$ exceeds
$-\frac2{27}P+\frac{5P-28}9\mu\ge\frac{37}{108}P-\frac73>0$, since $5P-28>0$
and $P\ge7$. If $q=5$ or $q=7$, then $(\mu_0,\mu_\infty,\mu_1)$ is
$(\frac35,\frac23,\frac{17}{25})$ or $(\frac57,\frac34,\frac{37}{49})$. On each of
the pieces of $\mathcal F_a$, $r$ is constant or an affine function of $\mu$ or
of~$m$, so the difference of the two sides of~\eqref{eq:Fscalar} is affine in
$\mu$ for fixed~$m$ and in $m$ for fixed~$\mu$; hence it is positive on the piece
if it is positive at the end points of the segment or at the four corners of the
rectangle. This is checked by exact rational arithmetic. (Lean:
\texttt{family\_bounds}, \texttt{family\_min}, \texttt{mu\_zero\_lower},
\texttt{scalar\_5}, \texttt{scalar\_7}, \texttt{scalar\_large},
\texttt{scalar\_F}.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:F}
Let $a\ge3$ and let $q=5$, $q=7$ or $q\ge8$ be real. Then (F$_a$) holds at~$(3,q)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $c\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$, $c\ne-s_a$, and $(\mu,m,r)\in\mathcal F_a$; we show
$\psi=\psi_\mu(c,ms_a)>2x\delta_a(3)r$. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:Fscalar}
and~\ref{lem:deficit3}(iii), $2x\delta_a(3)r\le\frac{41}{27}Lxr$, $0<xr<P$,
$0\le\mu\le1$ and $\frac35\le m\le1$; and $ms_a\in[-1,1]^{a+1}$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:gain} applies.

\emph{Case 1: $c_{a-1}=c_a$.} Then $\Delta(c)\ge2\delta_a(3)$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit3}(i), and Lemma~\ref{lem:gain} gives
$\psi\ge P\Delta(c)\ge2P\delta_a(3)>2x\delta_a(3)r$.

\emph{Case 2: $c_a=(s_a)_a$ and $c_{a-1}=(s_a)_{a-1}$.} By
Lemma~\ref{lem:lastpair} with $w=s_a$, $\psi\ge4(P-\mu)mL$. Since
$4(P-\mu)m\ge\frac{12}5(P-1)>\frac{41}{27}P>\frac{41}{27}xr$ for $P\ge4$, we get
$\psi>\frac{41}{27}Lxr\ge2x\delta_a(3)r$.

\emph{Case 3: $c_a=-(s_a)_a$ and $c_{a-1}\ne c_a$.} As $c\ne-s_a$, the largest
index $J$ with $c_J=(s_a)_J$ exists, and $J<a$. If $J=a-1$, then
$c_{a-1}=(s_a)_{a-1}=-(s_a)_a=c_a$, which is excluded; so $J\le a-2$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:alttail} applies to $w=c$. With its notation, $J\in\NA(c)$ and
$|\lambda_J(c)|=\theta$, so $\operatorname{pen}_i(c)=2L\theta$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:pen}; the cell $h_i$ has the arguments $\varepsilon L(1-\theta)$ and
$(M ms_a)_i=\varepsilon mL(1+\theta)$, and $1-\theta\le m(1+\theta)$ because
$m\ge\frac35$ and $\theta\ge\frac13$. By~\eqref{eq:hsame},
$h_i=2\mu mL(1+\theta)-2PL(1-\theta)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:gain}, with $K(\mu)\ge0$ and
$\Delta(c)\ge L\theta$, gives
\[
\psi\ge L\bigl[K(\mu)\theta+4\mu\theta-2\mu m(1+\theta)+2P(1-\theta)\bigr]
=L\bigl[2P-P(1-\mu)\theta-2\mu m(1+\theta)\bigr].
\]
The bracket decreases in $\theta$, and $\theta\le\frac59$, so
$\psi\ge L[2P-\frac59P(1-\mu)-\frac{28}9\mu m]>\frac{41}{27}Lxr$
by~\eqref{eq:Fscalar}.

If $c_{a-1}\ne c_a$ and $c_a=(s_a)_a$, then $c_{a-1}=-c_a=(s_a)_{a-1}$; so the
three cases cover every $c\ne-s_a$. (Lean: \texttt{full\_F},
\texttt{high\_deficit\_F}, \texttt{last\_pair\_scalar}, \texttt{tail\_F\_lower},
\texttt{tail\_gain\_algebra}.)
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:terminal}
Let $g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ with $g_a=-1$, $g\ne g_+$ and $\Delta(g)\le2\delta_a(3)$.
Then $g_{a-1}\ne g_a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $g_{a-1}=g_a$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit3}(i), the term $j=a-1$
of~\eqref{eq:Deltaid} equals $2\delta_a(3)$. Every other $j\in\NA(g)$ adds a
positive term, so $\NA(g)=\{a-1\}$. Then $g$ alternates on $0,\dots,a-1$, and
$g_{a-1}=g_a=-1$, so $g_i=(-1)^{a+i}$ for $i<a$ and $g_a=-1$, that is, $g=g_+$, a
contradiction. (Lean: \texttt{repeat\_before\_terminal\_impossible},
\texttt{terminal\_low}.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:C}
Let $a\ge3$ and let $q\ge5$ be real. Then (C$_a$) holds at~$(3,q)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $g\in\{\pm1\}^{a+1}$ with $g_a=-1$, $g\ne-(-1)^as_a$ and $g\ne g_+$, and
suppose that $\Delta(g)\le2\delta_a(3)$; we prove (C1)--(C3), with
$\gamma=2\delta_a(3)-\Delta(g)\ge0$. The only alternating sign vector with last
entry $-1$ is $-(-1)^as_a$, so $g\ne\pm s_a$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit3},
$\Delta(g)\ge\frac8{27}3^a>0$ and $2\delta_a(3)\le\frac{28}{27}3^a\le\frac72\Delta(g)$,
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
\gamma\le\tfrac52\Delta(g),\qquad 2\delta_a(3)<4\Delta(g).
\end{equation}
For $0\le y\le1$ the recursion $\eta_{n+1}(y)=(P-\eta_n(y))/x$ gives
$0\le\eta_n(y)\le1$ for all $n$, $\eta_n(y)\ge(P-1)/x=\mu_0$ for $n\ge1$,
$\eta_n(y)\le(P-\mu_0)/x=\mu_1$ for $n\ge2$, and $x\eta_1(y)=P-y$; also
$x\mu_1=P-\mu_0$ and $\mu_0\ge\frac35$. The sets $I_k$ and the intervals in (C2) lie
in $[0,1]$, and $I_k\subseteq(0,1]$ as $\mu_0>0$. (Lean: \texttt{q\_parameters},
\texttt{eta\_unit}, \texttt{eta\_lower\_mu}, \texttt{eta\_upper\_mu},
\texttt{q\_mu\_one}, \texttt{ik\_unit\_positive}, \texttt{gamma\_upper}.)

\emph{(C2).} Each of the five terms on the left is at least $P\Delta(g)$, and the
right side is at most $x\cdot\frac52\Delta(g)$ by~\eqref{eq:gamma}, as
$0\le\eta_5(y)\le1$. Since $5P-\frac52x=\frac52(x-2)>0$ and $\Delta(g)>0$, (C2)
holds. (Lean: \texttt{five\_chain}.)

\emph{(C3).} We have $\hat D_1=(3x-4)/x\ge2$ and
$\hat D_2=(5x^2-8x+4)/x^2\ge2$, since $(3x-2)(x-2)\ge0$, while $\mu_1\le1$ and
$\mu_\infty\le1$. So $\Delta(g)(\hat D_1/\mu_1+2)$ and
$\Delta(g)(\hat D_2/\mu_\infty+2)$ are at least $4\Delta(g)>2\delta_a(3)$
by~\eqref{eq:gamma}. If $a$ is odd, then $2\delta_a(3)=3^a-1$ and
$\Delta(g)\ge\frac13 3^a$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit3}(ii); as $\mu_0=(x-2)/x$, we
have $\hat D_1/\mu_0=(3x-4)/(x-2)>3$, so $\Delta(g)\hat D_1/\mu_0>3\Delta(g)\ge
3^a>2\delta_a(3)$. The bound $\Delta(g)\ge\frac8{27}3^a$ alone would not suffice
here, as $\hat D_1/\mu_0\to3$ when $x\to\infty$. (Lean:
\texttt{terminal\_ratios}.)

\emph{(C1) for $k\ge2$.} The column $\zeta=(-1)^k\mu_{k-1}g$ lies in
$[-1,1]^{a+1}$, so $\psi_y(c,\zeta)\ge P\Delta(c)\ge0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:gain}.
The terms $n=1,2$ of the sum are each at least $P(1+\mu_0)\Delta(g)$, and the
others are nonnegative. By~\eqref{eq:gamma} and $\eta_{k+1}(y)\le\mu_1$, the right
side is at most $\frac52x\mu_1\Delta(g)=\frac52(P-\mu_0)\Delta(g)$. As
$2P(1+\mu_0)-\frac52(P-\mu_0)=P(2\mu_0-\frac12)+\frac52\mu_0>0$, (C1) holds; in
fact for every $k\ge2$, every $y\in[0,1]$ and every~$c$. (Lean:
\texttt{chain\_sum\_two}, \texttt{c1\_long}.)

\emph{(C1) for $k=1$.} We must show
$P(1+\eta_1(y))\Delta(g)+\psi_y(c,-\mu_0g)>x\eta_2(y)\gamma$; we do so for every
$c$ and every $y\in[0,1]$. The first term is positive and the second nonnegative,
so we may assume $\gamma>0$. Recall that $x\eta_2(y)=P-\eta_1(y)\le P-\mu_0$.

If $c\ne\pm s_a$, then $\psi_y(c,-\mu_0g)\ge P\Delta(c)\ge\frac8{27}3^aP$ by
Lemmas~\ref{lem:gain} and~\ref{lem:deficit3}(ii), and with $\eta_1(y)\ge\mu_0$,
$\Delta(g)\ge\frac8{27}3^a$ and $2\delta_a(3)\le\frac{28}{27}3^a$ the left side
minus the right side is at least
\[
\Delta(g)\bigl[P(1+\mu_0)+P-\mu_0\bigr]+P\Delta(c)-(P-\mu_0)\,2\delta_a(3)
\ge\tfrac{3^a}{27}\bigl[4P(2\mu_0-1)+20\mu_0\bigr]>0.
\]
(Lean: \texttt{c1\_one\_nonalt}.)

If $c=\pm s_a$, then, as $\psi_y(-c,-\zeta)=\psi_y(c,\zeta)$,
$\psi_y(c,-\mu_0g)=\psi_y(s_a,\mu_0w)$ with $w=-g$ if $c=s_a$ and $w=g$ if
$c=-s_a$. Here $w\ne\pm s_a$ and $\Delta(w)=\Delta(g)<2\delta_a(3)$, so
$w_{a-1}\ne w_a$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:deficit3}(i). If $w_a=(s_a)_a$, then

gives $\psi_y(s_a,\mu_0w)\ge4(P-y)\mu_0L$; as $x\eta_2(y)<P$, $y\le1$ and
$\mu_0\ge\frac35$, we have $\frac{41}{27}x\eta_2(y)<4(P-y)\mu_0$ as in Case~2 of the
proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:F}, so the right side,
$x\eta_2(y)\gamma\le x\eta_2(y)\,2\delta_a(3)\le\frac{41}{27}Lx\eta_2(y)$, is smaller

\texttt{c1\_one\_alt\_positive}, \texttt{alt\_last\_pair\_gain},

\emph{(C1) for $k=0$.} Here $0<y\le1$, $x\eta_1(y)=P-y$ and $\mu_{-1}=1$, so we
must show $\psi_y(c,g)>(P-y)\gamma$ for every $c\ne-g$. By

\emph{Case A: $c\ne\pm s_a$.} If $\Delta(c)+\Delta(g)\ge2\delta_a(3)$, then
$\gamma\le\Delta(c)$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:gain} gives
$\psi_y(c,g)\ge P\Delta(c)>(P-y)\Delta(c)\ge(P-y)\gamma$, as $y>0$ and

\[
\psi_y(c,g)-(P-y)\gamma\ge K(y)\Delta(c)+2(P-y)L(1-\theta)

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:minfour} when one of the primes is~$3$.} If
$\min\{a,b\}\le2$, the theorem is Theorem~\ref{thm:onem} or~\ref{thm:ptwo}; so let
$\min\{a,b\}\ge3$. Exchanging the names of the primes together with their
exponents changes neither $n$, nor the claimed value, nor the two sets; so we may
assume $p=3$. Then $q\ge5$ is a prime, so $q=5$, $q=7$ or $q\ge11$; the point
$(3,q)$ lies in $\mathcal R_2$, and $P=q-1\ge4=P_{\min}$. By
Propositions~\ref{prop:S}, \ref{prop:F} and~\ref{prop:C}, (S$_a$) with
$P_{\min}=4$, (F$_a$) and (C$_a$) hold at $(3,q)$. By the pointwise form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}, every $Y\in\{\pm1\}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}$ with
$y_{ab}=-1$ satisfies $G(Y)\le\Theta$, where
$\Theta=d_a(3)\,d_b(q)-2\delta_a(3)\,\delta_b(q)$, with equality exactly for $Y^-$
and~$Y^+$. As in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ptwo},
$2\E(\ICG_n(\mathcal D))=n+G(Y)$ for the sign matrix $Y$ of a set $\mathcal D$ of
proper divisors of $n$, distinct sets have distinct sign matrices, and $Y^-$ and
$Y^+$ are the sign matrices of $\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{odd}\}$ and
$\{p^iq^j:\ i+j\ \text{even}\}\setminus\{n\}$, which are nonempty sets of proper
divisors of $n$. So the maximal energy is $\frac12(n+\Theta)$, attained exactly by
these two sets. (Lean: \texttt{prime\_domain}, \texttt{prime\_conditions},
\texttt{prime\_sign\_conclusion}, \texttt{CodexPaper1.Prop31.sign\_conclusion} of
version~2, \texttt{GraphTransport.graph\_result} and
\texttt{CodexPaper1.TaskE.check\_SameParityThree}.) \qed

\paragraph{What this section does not prove.} Condition (F$_a$) is proved at
$(3,q)$ only for $q=5$, $q=7$ and $q\ge8$, which is enough for the primes; it is not
claimed for $5<q<7$ or $7<q<8$. Condition (C$_a$) is proved on $\mathcal R_2$ and
(S$_a$) on both regions, but (F$_a$) and (C$_a$) are not claimed at any point of
$\mathcal R_1$ for $a\ge3$; so the case of Conjecture~\ref{conj:equal} with
$p,q\ge5$ and $\min\{a,b\}\ge3$ is not proved here. In particular we do not claim
that the three conditions hold at every point of both regions for every
$a\ge3$.

\texttt{UniformConditionsBothRegions}, that the three conditions hold at every point
of both regions for every $a\ge3$, is only a definition: it is not proved, not
claimed, and not used. \texttt{SameParityThreeMinEight} is a direct consequence of
\texttt{SameParityThree} and is not a separate result; no computer certificate is
used. The conditions are not claimed at any point of $\mathcal R_1$ with $a\ge3$,
apart from (S$_a$), so nothing here concerns the orders of equal parity with
$p,q\ge5$ and $\min\{a,b\}\ge3$. No Lean statement depends on~\cite{jy},
\cite{jy2}, \cite{ilic}, \cite{ilicbasic} or~\cite{lesander}, and attributions to
them occur only in comments of the statement file of version~2. The attributions, and the general results of Le
and Sander for arbitrary~$n$, are not formalised, and the comparison
\texttt{MultiplicativeMaxComparison} is only for orders $p^aq^b$. The statements say
nothing about priority or about the searches of Section~1. The agreement of the Lean
definitions with those of the paper was checked by the reviewing agents, not by a
machine. Formal proofs may follow other routes than the written ones.
