\begin{abstract}
Many entries of the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences contributed by
Paul D.~Hanna define a power series by a functional equation and conjecture a
congruence for its coefficients. We prove eleven such conjectures:
\begin{itemize}
\item the modulo-three conjecture of A389472;
\item the parity conjectures of A120566, A240998, A273958, A295762, A301933,
 A338633, A338634 and A388734;
\item the modulo-three conjectures of A274479 and A377100.
\end{itemize}
We also resolve three more:
\begin{itemize}
\item for A184894 and A107099 we prove the conjectured vanishing modulo~$3$
 outside the exceptional indices. We also show that the stronger reading,
 non-vanishing at every exceptional index, is false. It fails at the
 OEIS index $365$ of A184894, and at the coefficient of $x^{729}$ (OEIS
 index $364$) of A107099;
\item for A361047 we prove the intended statement, which the entry states
 with an index shift.
\end{itemize}
Several of these results are quick consequences of classical results, which
we point out. Modulo~$2$, the series of A240998, A295762, A301933, A338633 and
A338634 reduce, up to an additive constant, to the Catalan series, and that
of A273958, multiplied by~$x$, to $C(C(x^2))$, where $C$ is the Catalan series, so these parity patterns
follow from the classical parity of the Catalan numbers. The results for
A184894 follow quickly from the classical correspondence for linearised
polynomials over~$\F_3$. For A120566, A274479 and A388734, identities stated
in the entries themselves give short proofs. For A107099 and A361047 the
reduced series are classical series, identified by a routine uniqueness
argument. Only for the modulo-three conjecture of A389472 and for A377100 did
we find neither a proof dated before version~1 of this note (26 September 2026)
nor a classical derivation; later proofs of both have appeared, see
Section~\ref{sec:intro}.
The proofs reduce the functional equations modulo a prime and use the
Frobenius identity, formal derivatives, explicit solutions with an $x$-adic
uniqueness argument, and Lucas's theorem. Every theorem on a defining equation
is stated for every integer power series satisfying it, under the hypotheses
given there. All the mathematics of this paper, including the existence and
uniqueness of the normalised solutions and their first terms, is formally
verified in Lean~4; statements about the OEIS entries and the literature are
not. The proofs were found by AI coding agents working under the author's
direction; their role is described at the end of the paper. No human
mathematician has reviewed this paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Throughout, $A(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a(n)x^n\in\Z[[x]]$, $A'$ is its formal
derivative, and for a series $h$ with $h(0)=0$ we write $A(h)$ or $A\circ h$
for the composition $\sum_n a(n)h^n$. The results are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:summary}; each conjecture is stated in the corresponding
entry~\cite{oeis}. The theorems hold for every integer solution of the
defining equation, under the hypotheses stated in each theorem.

With the normalisations of Table~\ref{tab:norm}, the equation of each of the
thirteen series entries has exactly one solution in $\Z[[x]]$; for A273958 this
is one pair $(C,A)$, and for A338633 and A338634 one series $A$ admitting tails
as in Theorem~\ref{thm:parity}(4). The equation of A389472 does not determine
$a(2)$ ($A=x$ is also a solution: both sides are then $x^2+x^3$), and the entry takes $a(2)=1$; the equation
of A240998 forces $a(0)\in\{0,1\}$ (the constant terms give $a(0)^2=a(0)$), and the entry takes $a(0)=1$. In the Lean
proofs, integrality for A274479 comes from Lemma~\ref{lem:frob} modulo~$2$, and
for A107099 from writing $A=x+2B$. For the continued fractions, the tails exist as $x$-adic limits. So the theorems are not vacuous, and they apply
to these solutions. Table~\ref{tab:norm} also lists the first terms of each
solution.

Existence, uniqueness and these first terms are verified in Lean. That the
solution is the sequence of the entry rests on the entry's definition of the
sequence by the same equation; no Lean statement mentions the OEIS. The agreement
with further terms of the b-files rests on the numerical checks of
Section~\ref{sec:lean}, not on Lean.

\paragraph{Prior work.} The modulo-two conjecture of A389472 was proved
earlier by Perez Fontelles~\cite{pf389472} and, independently, in the
\texttt{trureturing} repository~\cite{tru389472}; those two proofs do not treat
the modulo-three conjecture.

\paragraph{Relation to classical results.} Several of the conjectures proved
here are quick consequences of classical results. Write
$C_n=\binom{2n}{n}/(n+1)$ for the Catalan numbers and
$C=\sum_{n\ge1}C_{n-1}x^n$ for the Catalan series, so that $C=x+C^2$.
Modulo~$2$, the series of A295762 and A301933, and those of A240998 (with
$a(0)=1$, as in the entry), A338633 and A338634 minus~$1$, all equal~$C$; this is how the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:parity} proceeds. For A301933 it can also be read off from
the remark in the entry that $C=x(1+2CC')/(1+CC')$: since
$2\equiv4\equiv0\pmod2$, both equations reduce to $F=x/(1+FF')$, which, with
$F(0)=0$, determines~$F$. Hence $a(n)\equiv C_{n-1}\pmod2$ for $n\ge1$, and these five
patterns follow from the classical fact that $C_n$ is odd if and only if
$n=2^k-1$, a corollary of $\nu_2(C_n)=s_2(n+1)-1$, where $\nu_2$ is the
$2$-adic valuation and $s_2$ the binary digit
sum~\cite[Theorem~2.1 in \dsarxiv]{ds}. Lemma~\ref{lem:dyadic} is this fact.
The continued fractions of A338633 and A338634 are the cases $e=3,4$ of a
family whose cases $e=1,2$ give, according to those entries, A000699 and
A158119. All of them reduce to
$A(A-1)=x$ modulo~$2$, and the parity of A000699 is recorded in A036987
(J.~M.~Campbell, 2016). For A273958, formula~(1) of the entry, which solves
the defining equation as a quadratic in $xA$, gives $xA=-C(-C^2)$, hence
$xA\equiv C(C(x^2))\pmod2$. The congruence
$C(C(x))\equiv\sum_{k\ge0}x^{4^k}\pmod2$ is also the key step of the proof of
Hanna's conjecture for A374568 in the \texttt{trureturing}
repository~\cite{tru374568}. Parts~(1) and~(2) of Theorem~\ref{thm:184894}
are an instance of the classical correspondence for linearised polynomials
over~$\F_3$, and the residues at the exceptional indices with $j\ge1$ of
A184894 and A107099 are, up to sign, Catalan numbers modulo~$3$; see the remark at the
end of Section~\ref{sec:additive}. For A361047 the reduced series coincides with that of the
ternary numbers A001764, whose residues modulo~$3$ are known; see
Remark~\ref{rem:361047}. For A120566, A274479 and A388734 we found no
classical connection, but identities stated in the entries themselves give
short proofs; see the remarks after Theorems~\ref{thm:388734},
\ref{thm:274479} and~\ref{thm:120566}.

Apart from the connections with classical results noted above, we found no
proof of any of the results in this paper dated before version~1 (26 September
2026) in the OEIS entries and their histories, in the literature, or in the
public repositories of proofs of OEIS conjectures that we searched (among
them~\cite{pfrepo,trurepo}). For the modulo-three conjecture of A389472 and for
A377100 we also found no classical connection. This reports the coverage of our
searches, not a guarantee of priority.

\paragraph{Later work.} After version~1 was released, the following proofs

\section{Tools}\label{sec:tools}
Reduction of the coefficients modulo a prime $p$ is a ring homomorphism
$\Z[[x]]\to\F_p[[x]]$; it commutes with formal differentiation and with
composition by series with zero constant term. We write $F=\overline A$ for
the reduction and $f(n)$ for its coefficients.

\begin{lemma}[Frobenius]\label{lem:frob}
For $F\in\F_p[[x]]$ we have $F(x)^p=F(x^p)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The map $G\mapsto G^p$ is a continuous ring endomorphism of $\F_p[[x]]$
that fixes $\F_p$ and sends $x$ to $x^p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fixed}
Let $K$ be a field, let $h\in xK[[x]]$ have order at least two, and let
$H\in K[[x]]$ satisfy $H(h)=H$. Then $H$ is constant.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $B=H-H(0)$, so $B(h)=B$. If $B\ne0$, its order $d$ is positive, while
$\ord B(h)\ge2d>d$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[dyadic recursion]\label{lem:dyadic}
Let $F\in\F_2[[x]]$ satisfy $F(0)=0$ and $F=x+F^2$. Then for $n\ge1$,
$f(n)=1$ if and only if $n$ is a power of~$2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:frob}, $F=x+F(x^2)$, so $f(n)=[n=1]+[2\mid n]f(n/2)$ for
$n\ge1$. By strong induction, $f(1)=1$, $f(n)=0$ for odd $n\ge3$, and
$f(2m)=f(m)$.
\end{proof}

Since the reduction of the Catalan series $C$ satisfies $F=x+F^2$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:dyadic} is the classical fact that $C_n$ is odd if and only if
$n=2^k-1$; see Section~\ref{sec:intro}.

We also use the following observation repeatedly: if $D\in\F_p[[x]]$,
$x^{n_0}\mid D$, and $x^n\mid D$ implies $x^{n+1}\mid D$ for all $n\ge n_0$,
then $D=0$.

\section{The modulo-three conjecture of A389472}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:389472}
If $A(x^2+x^3)=x^2(1+A(x))$, then $a(3n-1)\equiv0\pmod3$ for every $n>1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $F=\overline A\in\F_3[[x]]$ and $h=x^2+x^3$. Then $F(h)=x^2(1+F)$ and
$h'=2x+3x^2=-x$. Differentiating, $-xF'(h)=-x(1+F)+x^2F'$, so
$F'(h)=1+F-xF'$ after cancelling $-x$. Differentiating again, the terms $F'$
cancel and $-xF''(h)=-xF''$, so $F''(h)=F''$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fixed},
$F''$ is constant. For $n>1$ the coefficient of $x^{3n-3}$ in $F''$ is
therefore zero, and it equals $(3n-1)(3n-2)f(3n-1)=2f(3n-1)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Parity via the dyadic recursion}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:parity}
In each of the following cases, $a(n)$ is odd for $n\ge1$ if and only if $n$
is a power of~$2$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item (A240998) $A^2=x+A(x+2x^2)$;
\item (A295762) $a(0)=0$ and $A(x-2A(x^2))=x+A(x^2)$;
\item (A301933) $a(0)=0$ and $A(1+AA')=x(1+4AA')$;
\item (A338633, A338634) $A$ satisfies the continued fraction
 $1=A-x/(A-2^ex/(A-3^ex/(A-\cdots)))$ with $e=3$, resp.\ $e=4$, in the
 following sense: there are series $T_0,T_1,\dots$ with constant terms
 $\pm1$ such that $(A-1)T_0=x$ and $(A-T_k)T_{k+1}=(k+2)^ex$ for all
 $k\ge0$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover (A273958), if $C=x+C^2$ and $xA+x^2A^2=C^2$, then $a(n)$ is odd if
and only if $n=2\cdot4^k-1$ for some $k\ge0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) Modulo~$2$, $(x+2x^2)^k\equiv x^k$, so $F^2=x+F$. Then $G=F-f(0)$
satisfies $G(0)=0$ and $G^2=F^2-f(0)=x+G$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:dyadic}
applies to $G$.
(2) Modulo~$2$ the inner series reduces to $x$, so $F=x+F(x^2)=x+F^2$.
(3) Modulo~$2$ the equation reads $F+F^2F'=x$. In characteristic~$2$,
$F''=0$ because $n(n-1)$ is even. Differentiating gives $F'=1$, hence
$F=x+F^2$.
(4) Modulo~$2$, $(A-T_0)T_1=0$ with $T_1$ a unit, so $T_0=A$ and
$(A-1)A=x$. Then $B=A-1$ satisfies $B(0)=0$ and $B+B^2=x$, so $B=x+B^2$, and
$a(n)=b(n)$ for $n\ge1$.

For A273958, constant terms give $C(0)^2=0$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:dyadic},
$c=\overline C$ is the indicator of the powers of~$2$. Put $B=x\overline A$,
so that $b(n+1)=f(n)$ and $B+B^2=c^2$, i.e.\ $B(x)+B(x^2)=c(x^2)$. Hence
$b(n)=0$ for odd $n$ and $b(2m)=b(m)+c(m)$. The powers of~$2$ split into the
disjoint sets $\{4^j\}$ and $\{2\cdot4^k\}$, and strong induction gives
$b(n)=1$ exactly for $n=2\cdot4^k$.
\end{proof}

\section{Explicit solutions modulo a prime}\label{sec:explicit}
In the next results we exhibit the reduction $F$ explicitly and show that it
is the only solution by the $x$-adic observation of Section~2.

\begin{theorem}[A388734]\label{thm:388734}
If $A=1+xA^2+x^2(1-x)A^3$, then every $a(n)$ is odd.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$2$, $U=1/(1-x)$ is a solution: $1+(x+x^2)/(1-x)^2=1/(1-x)$ because
$x+x^2\equiv x(1-x)$. Subtracting the equations for $F$ and $U$ gives
$F-U=(F-U)\,x\,[F+U+x(1-x)(F^2+FU+U^2)]$, so $x^n\mid F-U$ implies
$x^{n+1}\mid F-U$. Hence $F=U$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark*}
Formula~(4) of the entry, read as a formula for $xA$ as in the program of
the entry, gives another short proof: $xA$ is the series reversion of
$\varphi=x(1-x-x^2)/(1-x^3)$. (This holds because $B=(1-x^3)/(1-x-x^2)$
satisfies $B=1+\varphi B^2+\varphi^2(1-\varphi)B^3$ with $\varphi=x/B$; since
$\varphi$ has no constant term, the equation $Y=1+\varphi Y^2+\varphi^2(1-\varphi)Y^3$
has a unique power-series solution, so $A(\varphi)=B$, which is formula~(5)
of the entry.) Since $1-x^3=(1-x)(1+x+x^2)$, we have
$\varphi\equiv x/(1+x)\pmod2$, whose reversion is $x/(1-x)$; hence
$A\equiv1/(1-x)$.
\end{remark*}

\begin{theorem}[A377100]\label{thm:377100}
If $a(0)=0$, $a(1)=1$ and $A=A(x^3)/A(x^2)+A^2$, then $a(n)\equiv1\pmod3$
for $n\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$3$ the equation reads $F(x^2)(F-F^2)=F(x^3)$. The series $E=x/(1-x)$
is a solution: both sides equal $x^3/(1-x)^3$, because $(1-x)^3=1-x^3$ and
$1-2x\equiv1+x$. For $D=F-E$ one computes
$E(x^2)\,D\,(1-F-E)=D(x^3)-D(x^2)(F-F^2)$. If $x^n\mid D$ with $n\ge2$, the
right-hand side is divisible by $x^{2n+1}$; since $E(x^2)$ is $x^2$ times a
unit and $1-F-E$ is a unit, $x^{2n-1}\mid D$. As $x^2\mid D$, we get $F=E$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[A274479]\label{thm:274479}
If $a(0)=0$, $a(1)=1$ and $A^2=A\bigl(x^2/(1-2x-4x^2)\bigr)$, then
$a(n)\equiv1\pmod3$ for $n\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$3$, $h=x^2/(1+x-x^2)$ and $E=x/(1-x)$ satisfies
$E(h)=x^2/(1+x-2x^2)=x^2/(1-x)^2=E^2$. For $D=F-E$ we have $D(F+E)=D(h)$. If
$x^n\mid D$ then $x^{2n}\mid D(h)$, and $F+E=xW$ with $W(0)=2$ a unit, so
$x^{2n-1}\mid D$. As $x^2\mid D$, we get $F=E$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark*}
The entry already notes, in its comment~(1), that $x/(1-x)$ satisfies
$F^2=F\bigl(x^2/(1-2x+2x^2)\bigr)$. As $-4\equiv2\pmod3$, this is the reduced
equation, so with this identity the conjecture has a short proof: only the
uniqueness argument above is needed.
\end{remark*}

\begin{theorem}[A120566]\label{thm:120566}
If $a(0)=0$, $a(1)=1$ and $A=A\circ A-x\,A\circ A\circ A$, then $a(n)$ is odd
for every $n\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$2$, $E=x/(1-x)$ satisfies $E\circ E=x/(1-2x)=x$, so
$E\circ E-xE\circ E\circ E=x-xE=E$. Let $D=F-E$ and suppose $x^n\mid D$ with
$n\ge2$. Since composition preserves congruences modulo $x^n$ and
$F=x+O(x^2)$, we have $D\circ F\equiv D$,
$E\circ F-E\circ E=D/((1-F)(1-E))\equiv D$, and
$x(F\circ F\circ F-E\circ E\circ E)\equiv0$, all modulo $x^{n+1}$. Since
$F\circ F-E\circ E=D\circ F+(E\circ F-E\circ E)$, subtracting the equations
for $F$ and $E$ gives $D\equiv2D\equiv0\pmod{x^{n+1}}$. As $x^2\mid D$, we get
$F=E$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark*}
Formula~(2) of the entry, $A(-A(-x))=x$, gives a shorter proof. It follows
from the defining equation: both $-A(-x)$ and any compositional inverse of~$A$
with zero constant term satisfy $B=B\circ B+x\,B\circ B\circ B$, and this equation has only
one solution $B=x+O(x^2)$. Modulo~$2$ we have $-A(-x)\equiv A$, so
formula~(2) gives $F\circ F=x$, and the defining equation becomes $F=x-xF$,
that is, $F=x/(1+x)$.
\end{remark*}

\begin{theorem}[A361047]\label{thm:361047}
If $A(0)=0$ and $A(x-x^3A'^2)=x$, then $[x^m]A\equiv1\pmod3$ when $m$ is a
power of~$3$, and $[x^m]A\equiv0\pmod3$ otherwise.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$3$ let $S=\sum_{k\ge0}x^{3^k}$. Then $S'=1$ and, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:frob}, $S-S^3=S-S(x^3)=x$, so $g\circ S=x$ for $g=x-x^3$.
Hence $S\circ g=x$, i.e.\ $S(x-x^3S'^2)=x$. For $D=F-S$ with $x^n\mid D$, the
inner series $g_F=x-x^3F'^2$ and $g_S=x-x^3S'^2$ differ by $x^3D'(F'+S')$,
which is divisible by $x^{n+2}$. Therefore
$0=F(g_F)-S(g_S)=D(g_F)+\bigl(S(g_F)-S(g_S)\bigr)\equiv D\pmod{x^{n+1}}$.
Hence $F=S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:361047}
The entry A361047 writes $A=\sum_{n\ge1}a(n)x^{2n-1}$ and conjectures
$a(n)\equiv1\pmod3$ exactly for $n=(3^k-1)/2$. With this indexing the
statement is contradicted by the data ($a(2)=1$). It is correct for the
index $i=n-1$, since $x^{2i+1}$ with $i=(3^k-1)/2$ is $x^{3^k}$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:361047} contains this intended statement.

Over~$\Z$ the reversion of $x-x^3$ is $V=\sum_{k\ge0}t(k)x^{2k+1}$, where
$t(k)=\binom{3k}{k}/(2k+1)$ are the ternary numbers (A001764), since
$V-V^3=x$. Modulo~$3$ this reversion is $S$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:361047} says
that $a(n)\equiv t(n-1)\pmod3$. The residues of $t(k)$ modulo~$3$ form
A113047, which is $1$ for $k=(3^j-1)/2$ and $0$ otherwise; this was
conjectured by P.~Barry and proved by K.~Ryde in 2021~\cite{ryde}. The
statement in A361047 is this pattern without the index shift. The remaining
step, $F=S$, or equivalently $F'=1$, which reduces the defining equation
modulo~$3$ to $F(x-x^3)=x$, follows by the routine uniqueness argument in the
proof above.
\end{remark}

\section{Additive series modulo three}\label{sec:additive}
Call $G\in\F_3[[x]]$ \emph{additive} if $[x^m]G=0$ whenever $m$ is not a
power of~$3$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:frob}, $G^{3^j}=G(x^{3^j})$, so the
composition of two additive series is additive.

\begin{theorem}[A184894]\label{thm:184894}
Let $P_0=x$, $P_{m+1}=P_m+P_m^3$, and $a(m)=[x^{2m-1}]P_m$ for $m\ge1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $2m-1$ is not a power of~$3$, then $3\mid a(m)$.
\item If $2m-1=3^j$, then $a(m)\equiv\binom mj\pmod3$.
\item $3\mid a(365)$, although $2\cdot365-1=3^6$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$3$, $P_{m+1}=P_m+P_m(x^3)$, so the coefficients $c(m,k)=[x^k]P_m$
satisfy $c(m+1,k)=c(m,k)+[3\mid k]\,c(m,k/3)$ with $c(0,k)=[k=1]$. By
induction, $c(m,k)=0$ unless $k$ is a power of~$3$, and
$c(m,3^j)=\binom mj$. Finally $\binom{365}{6}\equiv0\pmod3$ by Lucas's
theorem, since $365=(111112)_3$ and $6=(20)_3$.
\end{proof}

The entry conjectures that $a(m)\equiv0\pmod3$ except at $m=(3^n+1)/2$.
Part~(1) proves this statement as written, namely vanishing outside the
exceptional indices. Part~(3) shows that the stronger reading, that $a(m)$ is
nonzero modulo~$3$ at every exceptional index, is false; the first failure is
at $n=6$. By Lucas's theorem, $a((3^n+1)/2)\not\equiv0$
exactly when every base-$3$ digit of $n$ except the units digit is at most~$1$.

\begin{theorem}[A107099]\label{thm:107099}
Let $A(0)=0$, $[x^1]A=1$ and $A\circ A=x+4x^3$. Then $3\mid[x^n]A$ whenever
$n$ is not a power of~$3$, and $3\mid[x^{729}]A$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Modulo~$3$ the equation reads $F\circ F=x+x^3$. Suppose $m$ is not a power
of~$3$ and $f(j)=0$ for all non-powers $j<m$. Let $T$ be the truncation of
$F$ below degree $m$; it is additive, so $[x^m]T\circ T=0$. Since
$F=T+f(m)x^m+O(x^{m+1})$ and $f(1)=1$, we get $[x^m]F\circ F=2f(m)$, while
$[x^m](x+x^3)=0$; hence $f(m)=0$. Thus $F=\sum_kc_kx^{3^k}$ is additive, and
comparing coefficients of $x^{3^k}$ in $F\circ F=\sum_ic_iF(x^{3^i})$ gives
$c_0=1$ and $2c_k+\sum_{0<i<k}c_ic_{k-i}=[k=1]$ for $k\ge1$. This yields
$(c_1,\dots,c_6)=(2,1,1,2,1,0)$.
\end{proof}

The entry writes $A=\sum_{n\ge0}a(n)x^{2n+1}$ and conjectures that
$[x^n]A\equiv0\pmod3$ except at $n=3^k$. Theorem~\ref{thm:107099} proves this
statement as written. It also shows that the stronger reading, non-vanishing
at every power of~$3$, is false: $[x^{729}]A=a(364)\equiv0\pmod3$. The OEIS
b-file of A107099 already shows $a(364)\equiv0\pmod3$.

\begin{remark*}
Parts~(1) and~(2) of Theorem~\ref{thm:184894} are an instance of the
classical correspondence between linearised polynomials over~$\F_3$ and
their conventional $3$-associates, $\sum_jc_jx^{3^j}\leftrightarrow
\sum_jc_jt^j$, under which composition corresponds to
multiplication~\cite{ore},~\cite[Lemma~3.59]{ln}: since $x+x^3$ corresponds
to $1+t$, we get $P_m\equiv\sum_j\binom mjx^{3^j}\pmod3$. Likewise, the
correspondence, which extends to series, shows that the additive series
$\sum_j\binom{1/2}{j}x^{3^j}$, which corresponds to $(1+t)^{1/2}$, composes
with itself to $x+x^3$ modulo~$3$. By a routine uniqueness argument the
reduction $F$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:107099} is this series. For $j\ge1$ we have
$\binom{1/2}{j}=2(-1)^{j-1}C_{j-1}/4^j\equiv(-1)^jC_{j-1}\pmod3$, and
$\binom{(3^j+1)/2}{j}\equiv\binom{1/2}{j}\pmod3$, because modulo~$3$ the series
$(1+t)^{(3^j+1)/2}$ and $(1+t)^{1/2}$ both have constant term~$1$ and both square
to $1+t$ modulo~$t^{3^j}$, so they agree below degree~$3^j$, and $j<3^j$. So in both entries the residue at
an exceptional index with $j\ge1$ is $(-1)^jC_{j-1}$ modulo~$3$.
By~\cite[Theorem~5.2 in \dsarxiv]{ds}, it is nonzero exactly when every
base-$3$ digit of $j$ except the units digit is $0$ or~$1$. Both failures come from
$3\mid C_5=42$.
\end{remark*}
